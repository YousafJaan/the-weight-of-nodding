# Episode 26: The 4 : 30 AM Winter Fog
### The First Passenger Van, Frostbitten Knuckles, and The Long Road to Multan

**Season 3 : The Mantle of Light** | Words: 736

> **Synopsis:** Because hostel residence was forbidden by family decree, Rija undertakes a punishing daily four-hour commute through dense winter fog on public transport, facing physical exhaustion and harassment with unshakeable resolve.

---

The brutal compromise that allowed my enrollment in the MPhil program came with a severe condition imposed by Father : I was strictly forbidden from residing in any hostel in Multan. I was permitted to pursue my postgraduate studies only as a day scholar, commuting daily from our rural homestead to Bahauddin Zakariya University and returning before the evening call to prayer.
 
This meant that my life was surrendered to the ruthless tyranny of the regional transport schedule.
 
Every single morning throughout the harsh, freezing winter of Southern Punjab, my alarm clock shattered the darkness at precisely four-fifteen.
 
While the village was submerged in pitch-black silence and the temperature hovered near freezing, I crept out from beneath my quilt into the unheated room. With numb, stiff fingers, I washed with icy water from the courtyard hand-pump, performed my Tahajjud and Fajr prayers, and dressed in three layers of warm sweaters beneath my heavy black woolen chador.
 
Mother would be waiting in the small, soot-stained kitchen, blowing on cow-dung embers to heat an iron cup of thick, sweet buffalo milk.
 
"Drink it quickly, my brave girl," she would whisper, adjusting my woolen scarf over my ears, her breath forming white clouds in the freezing air. "The wagon will arrive at the crossroads in twenty minutes. Keep your spray of pepper seeds in your pocket, and do not make eye contact with the conductor."
 
By four-forty-five, I was standing alone on the gravel shoulder of the main rural artery, swallowed by a blinding, impenetrable white fog that rolled off the irrigated wheat fields. You could not see five yards ahead. The only sound was the distant howling of jackals and the dull thud of heavy transport trucks hurtling through the mist like blind behemoths.

When the first commercial wagon emerged from the fog : a battered, rattling Toyota HiAce van adorned with garish neon stickers and blaring high-pitched regional folk music : it was already crammed to double its legal capacity.
 
As the lone female passenger boarding at that desolate rural crossing, the daily ordeal was an exercise in pure survival.
 
The conductor would shove me into the cramped front bench beside the driver, wedged between a chain-smoking middle-aged tobacco trader and an irritable police constable whose service revolver dug painfully into my hip with every pothole.
 
The journey took nearly two hours each way. The windows were permanently frosted over, trapping the nauseating stench of stale diesel fumes, wet wool coats, cheap bidi smoke, and damp mud. My fingers were so numb with cold that I could barely turn the pages of my constitutional law treatises, yet I forced my eyes to read by the dim, vibrating amber light of the vehicle's dashboard.
 
Men on those public vans in rural Pakistan possess an insidious, predatory habit of shifting their knees, pretending to doze off, allowing their shoulders and elbows to invade a woman's physical space.
 
On my third week, a burly landlord seated beside me began deliberately pressing his knee against my thigh, pretending to read a newspaper while staring at my profile.
 
I did not cower. I did not shrink against the door in tears.
 
I pulled my long steel hair needle from my bun, held its sharp point between my thumb and forefinger, and when his knee pressed against me again around a sharp turn, I drove the steel needle three millimeters into the meat of his thigh!
 
The man let out a sharp, choked shriek of pain, leaping in his seat!
 
"Is there a problem, brother?" I asked, looking directly into his shocked, pale face with cold, crystalline malice, the needle gleaming openly in my hand. "Perhaps you have restless leg syndrome. If your limb twitches toward my seat again, I will ensure it requires surgical stitches."
 
The passenger in front of us snickered. The man shrank into his corner, pulling his shawl tightly over his knees, and did not move an inch for the remainder of the journey.
 
By seven-fifteen, the van would deposit me at the Bosan Road terminal. My bones ached, my eyes burned from diesel smoke, and my toes were frozen into blocks of ice. But as I walked through the grand university gates and caught sight of the Central Library dome rising above the morning mist, my spirit soared.
 
Every mile of that grueling road was a toll paid for my intellectual freedom, and I paid it with pride.
