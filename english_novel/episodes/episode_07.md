# Episode 07: Secret Flames and Stolen Dough
### The Dining Hall Boycott, Midnight Bread, and Preserving Self-Respect

**Season 1 : The Sanctuary and the Iron Gate** | Words: 968

> **Synopsis:** Facing inedible, contaminated food and public humiliation in the hostel mess, Rija leads a silent boycott, forcing Room 42 to establish a high-stakes clandestine kitchen beneath the warden's nose.

---

The hostel mess was operated by a private contractor named Bashir, a coarse, belligerent individual whose culinary ethics were as nonexistent as the hostel's heating budget. The food served to three hundred residential daughters was barely fit for livestock : the yellow lentil soup was so diluted that one could clearly see the aluminum pattern at the bottom of the bowl, while the flatbreads were so dry, burnt, and petrified that chewing them required the jaw strength of a carnivore.
 
Following the fallout of the HEC research appointment, the dining hall staff : who regularly received tips and discarded designer garments from Umme Rubab's entourage : adopted an openly mocking, insolent posture toward me. Whenever I stood in the dinner queue with my tin plate, the server would deliberately ladle the watery dregs of the pot onto my tray, tossing two blackened, rock-hard pieces of bread with an audible sneer.
 
The breaking point arrived on a stifling Thursday evening. As I approached the distribution counter, the greasy steam rising from the vegetable stew carried the unmistakable, nauseating odor of industrial kerosene.
 
"Brother," I said, addressing the server with controlled politeness, "this stew smells heavily of fuel. It is contaminated and unsafe to consume."
 
The man slammed his steel ladle against the rim of the cauldron, stepped forward, and spat onto the damp floor beside my feet. "Listen to the scholar bibi!" he shouted, ensuring that every girl in the dining hall heard his words. "This is a government subsidized hostel, not a five-star hotel in Lahore! If you are so delicate that you cannot digest our food, go back to your father's mud house and feast on butter! Here, you eat what is served, or you starve!"
 
A ripple of cruel, muffled titters echoed across the long dining tables. My face burned with humiliation, but I refused to give them the satisfaction of a scene. I carefully set the tin tray upon the counter, turned around without uttering a single syllable, and walked out into the courtyard. That night, I swore a sacred oath to myself : even if starvation clawed my intestines apart, I would never again swallow a single grain of food that came wrapped in public insult.

Yet, the human body cannot survive on moral indignation alone. After three consecutive days of subsisting on municipal tap water and two dry biscuits purchased from the corner tuck shop, dizzy spells began to blur my vision, and black spots danced across my research notes whenever I stood up.
 
Once again, the pragmatic sisterhood of Room 42 mobilized for survival.
 
We pooled our meager coins : Sara contributed three hundred rupees, Anza added a crumpled fifty-rupee note, and I threw in the last two hundred rupees remaining from my emergency fund. The following morning, Sara undertook a covert mission to Sadar Bazaar, returning with a contraband blue camping gas cylinder, a miniature cast-iron griddle, and a two-kilogram bag of white flour.
 
Smuggling these combustible items past the security post was an operation requiring nerves of steel. Sara carried the heavy iron griddle strapped against her back beneath a voluminous wool shawl, while I distracted the elderly gatekeeper by engaging him in an elaborate, confusing inquiry regarding postal parcel delivery schedules.
 
By eleven at night, when the corridor lights were extinguished and Aisha Hall fell into silence, Room 42 transformed into a clandestine midnight bakery. We wedged damp bath towels along the crack beneath the door to prevent the slightest whiff of hot dough from escaping into the hall.
 
Anza kneaded the flour in a plastic laundry basin; I sat cross-legged upon a bedsheet on the floor, using a clean glass cold-drink bottle as a makeshift rolling pin to flatten small, circular rotis; while Sara crouched over the hissing blue flame of the miniature burner, carefully turning the bread until it puffed with fragrant, golden blisters.
 
When the aroma of fresh, hot bread filled our tiny, suffocating room, it felt like an unspeakable luxury. We dipped the steaming bread into a paste made of chopped raw onions, salt, and green chilies. Hot tears leaked down our cheeks as we ate : tears not of sorrow, but of intense, defiant gratitude.
 
Suddenly, a heavy, aggressive banging hammered against the doorframe, making our hearts leap into our throats!
 
"Open this door immediately!" screeched the high-pitched voice of the night duty maid. "I smell gas in the corridor! You are cooking illegally! I am summoning Miss Nazia this instant!"
 
Panic exploded in the room. Anza froze with dough on her fingers; Sara acted with terrifying presence of mind. She instantly shut off the gas valve, kicked the hot griddle beneath an empty iron trunk, grabbed a bottle of kerosene from her lamp kit, and sprinkled five drops onto the floor tiles, simultaneously igniting a thick bundle of lemongrass mosquito coils.
 
When Sara threw open the door, a dense cloud of fragrant white herbal smoke billowed directly into the maid's face, sending her into a violent coughing fit.
 
"What on earth is wrong with you, Ayah ji?" Sara barked with imperial fury, hands on her hips. "The drainage ditch outside this window is breeding swarms of dengue mosquitoes! We are burning lemongrass and incense to prevent malaria, and you come screaming about gas? Do you want us to die of fever?"
 
The maid peered suspiciously through the haze, sniffed the overwhelming lemon aroma, and grumbled incoherently before retreating down the stairwell.
 
The moment the latch clicked shut, the four of us collapsed onto the floor, clutching our stomachs, laughing in silent, breathless hysteria. Yet within moments, that laughter dissolved into solemn quiet. We were not criminals; we were not thieves. We were merely young women fighting for the basic dignity of survival in an environment designed to crush our spirits at every turn.
