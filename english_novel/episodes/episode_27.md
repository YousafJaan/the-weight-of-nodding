# Episode 27: Penning for Bread
### The Regional Op-Eds, The Postal Money Order, and The Independent Voice

**Season 3 : The Mantle of Light** | Words: 687

> **Synopsis:** To cover her mounting daily transport and book expenses, Rija begins writing hard-hitting analytical columns on rural women's constitutional rights for regional newspapers, earning her first independent intellectual income.

---

While the tuition academy in my courtyard covered the fixed university semester fees, the daily operational costs of postgraduate education were unrelenting : sixty rupees for the round-trip passenger wagon, twenty rupees for photocopied judicial transcripts, and thirty rupees for a plate of boiled chickpeas at the student canteen.
 
My clay savings vessel was exhausted, and I refused to ask Mother for another scrap of silver.
 
It was during my second semester that I realized my greatest economic asset was not my physical labor, but the black ink flowing from my pen.
 
Southern Punjab was in the grip of a silent social crisis : honor killings, forced marriages, and the denial of women's land inheritance were rampant across the Saraiki belt, yet regional Urdu journalism rarely addressed these tragedies beyond sensationalist crime briefs buried on page four. The editorial pages were dominated by retired civil servants writing stale, verbose essays on national political intrigues.
 
Using an antique manual Urdu typewriter borrowed from the village postmaster, I began drafting 1,200-word analytical columns under the column title : *'From the Courtyard to the Courtroom'* (*دہلیز سے عدالت تک*).
 
My first piece was an incisive, legally grounded critique of the Punjab Fair Representation of Women Act, exposing how bureaucratic loopholes allowed feudal landlords in Rajanpur to transfer agricultural land titles to fictitious female relatives while denying actual daughters their rightful tenancy.
 
I mailed the manuscript to the editorial office of *Daily Nawa-i-Waqt* in Multan, accompanied by a polite handwritten cover letter.
 
Two weeks passed without response. I had almost dismissed the effort as naive foolishness.
 
Then, on a crisp October morning, the village postman pedaled his bicycle into our courtyard, ringing his brass bell with unusual enthusiasm.

The postman, an elderly man named Chacha Ramzan who had known my father for thirty years, held out an official government postal money order coupon and a fresh copy of the morning newspaper.
 
"Is Sajjad Sahib home?" the postman asked, peering through his spectacles.
 
"Father is at the fields, Chacha," I said, stepping forward. "Is there a letter for him?"
 
"No, daughter," Chacha Ramzan smiled, his weathered face creasing with immense pride. "The money order is not for your father. It is addressed to : *Miss Rija Sajjad, Research Scholar*. One thousand two hundred rupees from the Publications Department in Multan! And look at page five of the national editorial section!"
 
He unfolded the broadsheet newspaper.
 
There, spanning three wide columns across the center of the editorial page, printed beneath a bold headline, was my article : **'THE SHACKLES OF SILENCE : HOW FEUDAL CUSTOMS STRANGLE FEMALE INHERITANCE IN SOUTH PUNJAB'** 
**By Rija Sajjad, Department of Political Science, BZU Multan.**
 
Seeing my own name printed in clear, permanent lead typography upon the pages of a major national newspaper was a moment of transcendent elevation.
 
For centuries, the women of our bloodline had lived and died in complete anonymity : their births unrecorded, their voices silenced behind courtyard walls, their graves marked only by uncarved field stones. But on that morning, my voice had broken through the mud plaster, traveling into the drawing rooms of ministers, judges, and intellectuals across the province.
 
I signed the postal coupon with my own hand. Chacha Ramzan counted out twelve crisp hundred-rupee notes into my palm.
 
Mother stood beside the kitchen door, pressing the newspaper against her chest, weeping tears of pure joy.
 
"Look at this, Rija," she sobbed softly, kissing the printed headline. "Your words are on paper. No one can burn this in a hearth. No one can tear this from the sky."
 
Over the next six months, I became a regular analytical contributor, publishing two columns every month on rural legal reform, constitutional rights, and education policy.
 
The twenty-four hundred rupees I received monthly via money orders not only paid for every single bus ticket and research dossier, but allowed me to purchase my own second-hand legal textbooks and provide medicine for Mother.
 
I had discovered the ultimate secret of human emancipation : when a daughter transforms her private suffering into public literature, she ceases to be a victim of society; she becomes its conscience.
