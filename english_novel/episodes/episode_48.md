# Episode 48: The Dust of Mother's Feet
### The Ancestral Courtyard, The Gold at the Hearth, and The Prostration of Tears

**Season 5 : The Turban and the Dawn** | Words: 541

> **Synopsis:** Returning through the gates of the ancestral homestead, Rija places her presidential gold medal at the feet of her mother, performing a profound prayer of thanksgiving in the very courtyard where she once wept in despair.

---

The procession from the railway station to our ancestral home was a triumphant carnival of joy. The villagers escorted our open jeep with drumbeats, scattering rose petals along the unpaved alleys, while women peered from the flat mud roofs, waving colorful dupattas and singing traditional wedding ballads of victory.
 
When we arrived outside our wooden compound gate, Father motioned for the crowd to remain in the street.
 
"This threshold belongs to her mother," Father said gently.
 
I pushed open the heavy wooden gate and stepped into the quiet courtyard.
 
Mother was standing alone in the center of the dawan, beside the clay hearth where she had spent twenty-five years of her life. She wore her simple faded grey cotton shalwar kameez and her worn black headscarf, her hands trembling as she held the silver hook of her bare left ankle where her bridal anklet had once rested.
 
I walked across the courtyard gravel, my eyes overflowing with tears.
 
I reached up, unhooked the wide green silk ribbon from around my neck, and took the heavy twenty-four-karat National Gold Medal into both hands.
 
I knelt down directly before Mother upon the cold, hard earth.
 
I placed the golden medal upon the bare dust, resting its shining face directly over her calloused, work-worn feet, and I pressed my forehead against the earth beside her toes.
 
"Mother," I whispered, my voice breaking into deep, trembling sobs of love. "Take your silver back. Take the gold of the nation. This victory does not belong to me; it belongs to the dust of your feet."
 
Mother let out a breathless, joyful cry, dropping to her knees in the dirt, throwing her arms around my neck, pulling my face against her chest.

We wept together upon the ground : two women whose tears had watered the barren soil of this courtyard, holding each other beneath the vast, open Punjab sky.
 
"My brave child," Mother whispered through her sobs, running her rough fingers over the golden seal resting against her toes. "My lioness. Your grandmother in her grave is smiling today. You have vindicated every tear that was ever shed inside these walls."
 
Father walked over, kneeling beside us, placing his strong arms around both of us. The three of us knelt together upon the earthen floor of our homestead, forming an unbreakable circle of love and redemption.
 
After a long, sacred silence, I rose to my feet, retrieved my grandmother's green velvet prayer rug, and spread it in the center of the courtyard : directly upon the spot where Hamza had smashed my mobile phone, directly upon the spot where Father had torn my graduation certificate.
 
I faced the Qibla, raised my hands, and performed two units of the Prayer of Thanksgiving (*Salat-ush-Shukr*).
 
When my forehead touched the velvet rug in the final prostration, I remained there for a long time, weeping silently into the fabric.
 
There was no request left in my soul; there was no complaint; there was only an infinite, ocean-deep gratitude to the Sovereign of the Cosmos who had taken a destitute, slandered daughter from the edge of the grave and crowned her with honor before the entire world.
 
The courtyard was no longer a prison. It was a sanctuary of divine victory.
