# Episode 12: Cold Glances and the Locked Threshold
### The Ancestral Gateway, Paternal Silence, and The Hostile Courtyard

**Season 2 : The Courtyard of Chains** | Words: 808

> **Synopsis:** Arriving at the ancestral homestead, Rija is greeted not with celebration for her academic distinction, but with the chilling, hostile surveillance of an armed brother and a silenced mother.

---

When the wagon dropped me at the edge of the village dirt track, the afternoon shadows were stretching across the brick alleys like long, dark fingers. The village seemed frozen in an ancient, timeless stupor : water buffaloes wallowed in the stagnant village pond, elderly men sat upon rope beds smoking clay hookahs beneath the banyan tree, and young boys chased stray dogs through the dust.
 
Yet, the moment I carried my trunk past the grocer's corner, the customary village chatter died into a tense, whispering silence. Eyes peered at me from behind half-opened wooden gates; old women lowered their headscarves and muttered venomous prayers against modern corruption. In their eyes, I was not a daughter who had secured high academic honors; I was a dangerous anomaly, a girl who had lived unattended in the city, carrying the potential contagion of freedom.
 
I pushed open the heavy wooden door of our family courtyard. The familiar scent of burning cow-dung fuel, wet mud plaster, and sour curd enveloped me.
 
Mother was seated on a low wooden stool beside the clay hearth, peeling bitter gourds. The moment she caught sight of me, her knife dropped into the brass basin with a dull clatter. She rose quickly, her face a heartbreaking canvas of maternal joy strangled by absolute terror.
 
She embraced me tightly, burying her face into my neck. Her body was shaking with silent sobs, and her familiar scent of dried rose petals and cardamom was overwhelmed by the bitter sweat of panic.
 
"Rija, my poor child, you have arrived," she whispered frantically into my ear, her eyes darting nervously toward the closed wooden door of the formal drawing room across the courtyard. "Quickly, wash your face and stay inside the small storage pantry. Do not show your face in the dawan."
 
"Where is Father, Mother?" I asked, setting down my bag.
 
"Inside the baithak," she replied, her voice breaking. "Taya Hakim Khan and Aunt Shabana have been screaming since ten this morning. They have brought the village elders. They are telling your father that if he does not crush your head now, our entire ancestry will be excommunicated from the tribal brotherhood."
 
Before I could answer, the door of the drawing room swung open with a violent creak.

My brother Hamza stepped out into the blinding afternoon glare. At twenty-four, Hamza was the quintessential product of Southern Punjab's rural patriarchy : broad-shouldered, sporting a thick, oiled mustache, his gaze brimming with that toxic, performative arrogance that young men in our culture adopt to prove their masculinity.
 
Tucked prominently into the leather belt of his shalwar was the wooden grip of an unlicensed Russian semi-automatic pistol.
 
He stood with his legs apart, surveying my plain black chador and dusty shoes with visceral disgust.
 
"So, the city princess has finally deigned to return to the mud," he snarled, walking slowly toward me, his hands resting near the weapon. "Did you bring your fancy university medal to show the village dogs? Put that trunk down before I kick it into the manure heap."
 
"Hamza, I am your elder sister," I said, looking directly into his hostile eyes without flinching. "I have returned with an honorable degree from the province's highest institution. Lower your voice and speak with basic decency."
 
My composure infuriated him far more than tears would have. In his world, a woman existed only to cower, tremble, and beg.
 
He took two aggressive strides forward, his hand snapping out to violently seize my wrist, twisting it until a sharp, burning agony shot up to my elbow.
 
"Listen to me, you rebellious wretch," he hissed through clenched teeth, his hot, nicotine-stained breath hitting my face. "Your degree has been condemned by Taya Hakim Khan as toilet paper. You are inside our walls now. There are no professors, no female wardens, and no international human rights commissions to protect you here. You will do precisely as the elders command, or by God, I will bury you beneath the floor of this courtyard and tell the police that you died of cholera."
 
"Hamza! Release her!" Mother screamed, throwing herself between us, her trembling hands clawing at his iron grip. "She is your sister! Fear the wrath of Allah!"
 
Hamza shoved Mother backward with brutal indifference, sending her stumbling against the brick hearth, but he released my wrist with a venomous jerk.
 
"Consider that a polite warning," he muttered, turning back toward the drawing room. "When Father summons you inside, you will keep your head bowed and your mouth shut."
 
I stood in the center of the courtyard, cradling my bruised wrist. The pain in my flesh was nothing compared to the cold, crystalline clarity settling into my mind. The illusions were gone. My family home was no longer a home : it was an enemy fortification, and I was a prisoner of war.
