# Episode 28: Mentorship of Dr. Masood
### The Scholar's Office, The Challenged Proposal, and The Departmental Grant

**Season 3 : The Mantle of Light** | Words: 883

> **Synopsis:** Department Chair Dr. Masood recognizes Rija's brilliance, pushing her beyond safe academic platitudes to pursue a dangerous, groundbreaking thesis on feudal land grabbing and legal renunciation.

---

The academic faculty of Bahauddin Zakariya University was largely divided into two distinct factions : comfortable, risk-averse tenured professors who published derivative literature reviews to secure administrative promotions, and genuine scholars who understood that universities are meant to be beacons of social transformation.
 
Dr. Masood Ahmed, Chair of the Department of Political Science, belonged entirely to the latter category.
 
A graduate of Cambridge University who had returned to his native Southern Punjab three decades prior, Dr. Masood was a towering intellectual with an unruly mane of silver hair, worn tweed jackets, and an uncompromising intolerance for academic mediocrity. He was feared by lazy students and despised by corrupt university administrators because he refused to yield to political quotas.
 
In the second semester of my MPhil, every candidate was required to submit a formal research proposal defense before the departmental board.
 
Most of my classmates selected safe, sterile, non-controversial topics : 'A Comparative Analysis of Local Government Ordinances in Punjab (2001-2015)' or 'The Role of Media in Electoral Voter Turnout'.
 
When my turn arrived to present my proposal inside the faculty boardroom, I placed my typed manuscript upon the podium.
 
My proposed thesis title was an intellectual grenade : *'LEGALIZED THEFT : THE SOCIOLOGICAL MECHANISMS OF FORCED WRITTEN INHERITANCE RENUNCIATION (HAQ BAKHSHWANA) AMONG AGRARIAN FEMALES IN THE CHENAB BASIN.'*
 
The room grew instantly tense. Several senior professors shifted uncomfortably in their swivel chairs.
 
"Miss Rija," remarked Dr. Arshad, the senior faculty member who had previously backed Umme Rubab. "This topic is dangerously polemical. You are not writing an investigative journalism piece for a scandal magazine. This touches upon sensitive tribal customs and private family arrangements. It would be far wiser to focus on a safer constitutional theme, such as federal provincial financial awards."
 
I stood tall behind the podium, refusing to waver.
 
"With due respect, Professor," I replied evenly. "If the purpose of political science is merely to catalog financial awards while fifty-two percent of our agrarian population is systematically stripped of their fundamental economic rights under the threat of physical violence, then this department is merely an expensive graveyard of useless theories. Research must bleed with the reality of the people, or it has no right to call itself knowledge."
 
Silence descended upon the boardroom.

Dr. Masood had been sitting quietly at the head of the table, polishing his spectacles with a silk cloth. He slowly slipped them back over his ears, placed both hands flat upon the polished wood, and looked around the room.
 
"Gentlemen," Dr. Masood announced, his deep voice carrying absolute, authoritative finality. "For twenty years, this faculty has produced hundreds of beige, forgettable, dust-gathering dissertations that say nothing, challenge nothing, and change nothing. For the first time in a decade, a scholar has walked into this room with the courage to place her scalpel directly into the festering wound of this feudal society."
 
He turned his piercing, benevolent gaze directly toward me.
 
"Miss Rija, your proposal is approved in its entirety. And furthermore, as Chairman, I am designating myself as your primary research supervisor."
 
A collective murmur swept through the committee. Dr. Masood had not supervised an MPhil student personally in four years due to his intense international publication schedule.
 
Later that afternoon, he summoned me to his private office : a cavernous room smelling of antique leather bindings, pipe tobacco, and mountains of scholarly journals.
 
"Sit down, Rija," he said, pouring two cups of green tea from an iron kettle.
 
He leaned forward across his massive desk, his expression dead serious.
 
"You have chosen a magnificent, terrifying mountain to climb," he said quietly. "To document forced land renunciation means you will be stepping on the toes of powerful feudal families, corrupt revenue officials, and patriarchal tribal chiefs. They will attempt to intimidate you; they will spread rumors about you; they may even threaten your family. Are you truly prepared for this fire?"
 
"Dr. Masood," I answered, meeting his gaze without blinking. "My family has already pressed a loaded shotgun against my temple. They have already locked me inside a grain storeroom and barred their doors against my name. The fire you speak of is not a threat to me; it is the element in which I learned to breathe."
 
A faint, profound smile curved the corners of his lips. He nodded slowly, opened his center drawer, and pulled out a stamped official university document.
 
"This is an award letter from the Higher Education Research Directorate," Dr. Masood said, sliding the paper toward me. "I have secured a competitive research grant of eighty thousand rupees for your thesis fieldwork, covering your archival access, court filing fees, and travel expenses across the district."
 
I looked at the grant letter, feeling a lump of deep gratitude rising in my throat.
 
"Thank you, Sir," I whispered.
 
"Do not thank me, Rija," he replied, rising to shake my hand. "Thank your own unshakeable courage. Now go to the archives and bring me the truth that this province has spent two centuries trying to bury."
 
Walking out of the department that afternoon, I felt an army of intellectual giants standing behind my shoulders. I was no longer an isolated village girl fighting alone in the mud; I was a scholar armed with the institutional legitimacy of academia, ready to dissect the anatomy of oppression.
