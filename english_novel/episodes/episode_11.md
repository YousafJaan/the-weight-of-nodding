# Episode 11: Farewell to Aisha Hall
### The Packed Iron Trunk, The Silent Corridor, and The Ominous Text Message

**Season 2 : The Courtyard of Chains** | Words: 594

> **Synopsis:** Concluding two transformative years at university, Rija bids farewell to Room 42 and Aisha Hall, only to receive a threatening text from brother Hamza as the passenger van turns toward her ancestral village.

---

The final day at Aisha Hall unfolded beneath a heavy, leaden July sky that mirrored the profound turmoil within my chest. Two years had elapsed since that scorching September noon when Father had abandoned me upon the threshold of Bosan Road. Those two years had carved away every vestige of naive provincial timidity, replacing it with an intellectual steel tempered in the fires of academic combat, administrative spite, and nocturnal solitude.
 
I fastened the heavy brass padlock upon my old tin trunk, its paint now scratched and chipped from multiple room migrations, and stood alone in the center of Room 42.
 
The room was completely stripped of human presence. Sara had departed two days prior for Bahawalpur, her eyes unexpectedly glistening with unshed tears as she pressed an antique silver fountain pen into my hand. Anza had moved to a postgraduate hostel near the faculty, while Saba had returned to Layyah to await her wedding day. The four iron cots stood bare and skeletal against the lime-peeled walls.
 
I walked over to the window and touched the iron bars one final time. From this vantage, I had witnessed Multan's torrential monsoons, its suffocating dust storms, and the infinite stars of its summer nights. In this room, I had wept until my chest ached with starvation; in this room, I had drafted research papers that challenged the legal architecture of Southern Punjab's feudal dynasties.
 
Dr. Masood had signed my graduation clearance that morning with high commendations, handing me the university's gold-embossed completion certificate. "Rija," he had said solemnly, "your intellect is a rare gift in this province. But remember : the university was merely your armor-maker. The actual battlefield lies beyond the gates."
 
His prophetic words lingered in my thoughts as I hauled the heavy trunk down the stairwell and placed it into the trunk of a battered taxi heading toward the regional bus terminal.

The passenger wagon journey toward our ancestral village was a slow descent into claustrophobia. As the modern asphalt avenues of Multan surrendered to cracked single-lane tarmac, and then to unpaved dirt tracks cutting through endless stretches of yellowed cotton fields, the air grew increasingly heavy with familiar, suffocating agrarian silence.
 
Suddenly, my mobile phone : an inexpensive secondhand device purchased with my HEC research earnings : vibrated with a sharp, jarring chime.
 
It was a text message from my elder brother, Hamza.
 
The words on the small monochrome screen struck my eyes like jagged glass : *'Come directly home. Do not dare to visit anyone on the way. Taya Hakim Khan, Uncle Farooq, and Aunt Shabana have been seated with Father since morning. Final decisions regarding your life and marriage have been settled by the family elders. If you think your university papers will protect you from our ancestral decisions, you will learn your lesson before sunset.'*
 
A violent chill surged through my veins, freezing the blood in my fingers. I stared at the screen until the letters blurred.
 
Through the vibrating window of the wagon, I watched the distant mud brick walls and eucalyptus groves of our village draw nearer beneath the afternoon haze. The university had taught me international law, constitutional equity, and human dignity. But out here, beyond the canal bank, centuries of feudal tribalism held absolute court, and the elders were waiting with their sharpened knives of custom to carve up my future.
 
I tightened my grip on the canvas bag containing my degree and my mother's prayer rug. The battle for education was over; the brutal war for my very existence was about to begin.
