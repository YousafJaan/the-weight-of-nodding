# Episode 17: The Dungeon of Grain Sacks
### The Dark Storeroom, The Iron Padlock, and The Silence of the Pit

**Season 2 : The Courtyard of Chains** | Words: 735

> **Synopsis:** Stripped of her phone, books, and communication, Rija is locked inside the dark, unventilated grain storeroom by Hamza to break her will and enforce submission to Taya's decree.

---

The formal imprisonment began on the fourth day after Eid.
 
At dawn, while the village call to prayer was still echoing from the minarets, Hamza kicked open the door of the small pantry where I slept. Without a word, he grabbed my canvas bag, pulled out my mobile phone, and smashed it against the brick floor, grinding the display beneath the heel of his heavy leather shoe until the microchips were pulverized into dust.
 
"You have used this cursed machine to bring enough destruction to our family," he snarled. "Now you will live where you belong."
 
He seized my arm with brutal force and dragged me across the rear courtyard toward the old grain storeroom : a windowless, mud-plastered chamber that had served for generations as a repository for jute grain sacks, discarded wooden charpais, and sacks of chemical fertilizer.
 
The air inside hit me like a solid wall of dust, suffocating heat, and the sharp, chemical stench of pesticide tablets. The only source of illumination was a single, pencil-thin crack in the wooden ceiling where the mud mortar had eroded between the bamboo rafters.
 
"Hamza, you cannot do this!" I screamed, struggling against his iron grip. "This is unlawful imprisonment! Let me go!"
 
"You will remain in this hole until your tongue learns to recite the words *'I accept'* before Taya Hakim Khan and the nikah registrar," he spat, shoving me violently into the darkness.
 
I stumbled over a pile of rotting hemp ropes, crashing heavily onto the hard dirt floor.
 
The thick wooden door slammed shut, and the heavy iron padlock clinked into place with a sickening, metallic thud that sounded like the final clods of earth being shoveled onto a fresh grave.
 
In the absolute, suffocating blackness that enveloped me, the world simply ceased to exist. I beat my fists against the splintered wood until my knuckles were bloody and raw, screaming for Mother, screaming for justice, until my vocal cords shredded and my voice collapsed into dry, rattling sobs.

The days inside that dungeon blurred into a timeless, hallucinatory nightmare.
 
There was no clock, no calendar, no distinction between day and night save for the faint, dusty ray of light that crawled across the burlap sacks for three hours every afternoon before dissolving into darkness. Swarms of gnats and mosquitoes feasted upon my bare arms and face, and the heat during the afternoons rose to feverish, intolerable heights.
 
Once a day, usually in the late evening when Hamza was away at the village tavern, the iron latch would scrape softly, and Mother would slide a metal plate with a dry flatbread and a pinch of salt pickle through a three-inch gap, along with a plastic bottle of warm water.
 
Her face was never visible, but her broken, terrified whispers seeped through the crack like a mourning song : "Rija... my baby... please, my heart is tearing apart. Just say yes to Arslan. Your father will forgive you. You will die inside this darkness."
 
"Mother," I whispered through the wooden slats, my lips cracked with dehydration, "if I marry Arslan, I will die every single minute of every single day for the rest of my life. I would rather my body rot inside this dust than surrender my soul to an abuser."
 
During the third or fourth night of my confinement : I had lost all count of the calendar : a severe fever seized my body. My limbs began to convulse with violent chills, and my mind drifted into delirious, terrifying visions. I saw the burning asphalt of Bosan Road, I heard the whistle of Miss Nazia, I saw Aqsa being dragged into the white van, her screams echoing off the walls of the storeroom.
 
Yet, in the deepest depths of that delirium, something profound occurred. The terror that had governed my existence for twenty years : the terror of Father's disapproval, the terror of brotherly violence, the terror of village gossip : simply burned away like chaff in a furnace.
 
When a human being is stripped of everything : their freedom, their books, their light, their water, their name : they discover the indestructible diamond that lies at the core of their soul. That diamond cannot be crushed by iron locks or broken by village panchayats.
 
I sat in the dust, leaned my burning forehead against the rough burlap of a wheat sack, and drew my first truly free breath.
