# Episode 23: The Jute Mat Academy
### The Mud Courtyard, The Village Children, and The First Steps of Self-Reliance

**Season 3 : The Mantle of Light** | Words: 761

> **Synopsis:** To finance her MPhil tuition without relying on male family patronage, Rija establishes an informal outdoor academy in her courtyard for poor village laborers' children, earning their trust and their hard-won pennies.

---

To dream of academic glory while possessing zero material resources is the quickest route to madness unless one learns to transmute the dirt beneath one's feet into gold. The total fee for the first semester of the MPhil program was twenty-four thousand rupees : an astronomical fortune for an impoverished rural household where twenty rupees bought an evening's bread.
 
I refused to look toward Father's wallet, and seeking a bank loan in rural Punjab without male collateral was an impossibility.
 
Therefore, I looked around our village with fresh eyes.
 
Behind the grand mud-brick compounds of landlords like Taya Hakim Khan lay a sprawling underclass of impoverished tenant laborers, tractor drivers, brick-kiln workers, and village craftsmen. Their sons and daughters attended the dilapidated government primary and middle schools where teachers rarely showed up, leaving the children functionally illiterate, unable to construct an English sentence or solve basic mathematics.
 
The morning after my confrontation with Father, I swept the earthen floor of our rear courtyard, spread an old, frayed jute mat beneath the shade of the ancient berry tree, and borrowed an old wooden packing crate from the grocer to serve as a chalkboard, painting its surface with black wood varnish.
 
I walked through the laborers' quarters, speaking directly to the mothers who were washing clothes by the irrigation channel.
 
"I am starting an evening coaching academy in my courtyard," I told them. "I will teach your children English grammar, Urdu composition, and basic science. Two hours every afternoon."
 
The women looked at me with cautious, hesitant eyes. In their world, educated people from landed families never spoke to them except to give orders or demand agricultural labor.
 
"Bibi," said the wife of the village carpenter, twisting her frayed dupatta. "We wish for our children to learn, but my husband earns barely three hundred rupees a day at the wood saw. We cannot afford the fancy fees of city tutors."
 
"I do not want city fees, Auntie," I replied, taking her rough, weathered hand into mine. "Pay me whatever your heart can spare : fifty rupees a month, twenty rupees, or a dozen fresh eggs from your chickens. Just send your children with clean slates and open minds."
 
That very afternoon, six frightened, barefoot children arrived at my gate, clutching torn notebooks and slates.

Within three weeks, the Jute Mat Academy became a village phenomenon.
 
I poured every ounce of my university pedagogical training into those children. I did not rely upon the sterile, brutal rote-memorization common in Pakistani government schools; I taught them through storytelling, through competitive grammar games, through drawing maps in the dirt with wooden sticks. I taught the girls that their voices were sacred, and I taught the boys that respect for their sisters was the true definition of strength.
 
When the monthly school examinations took place at the government middle school, the headmaster was stunned : the top four ranks in both English and general science were swept by the children who studied on my jute mat!
 
The news spread across the village like wildfire. Mothers arrived at my courtyard leading their children by the hand, begging for enrollment. Within two months, more than thirty-five students gathered under that berry tree every afternoon from three to six.
 
Those three hours were physically grueling. The afternoon heat was often suffocating, swarms of flies circled the courtyard, and my vocal cords ached from projecting across thirty children for hours on end.
 
Yet, when the sun dipped below the horizon and the lesson concluded, the children would line up to show their work. Their dirty, eager faces glowing with the joy of understanding was the purest medicine for my wounded soul.
 
At the end of each month, the mothers arrived. They would untie small knots in the corners of their dupattas, producing crumpled, sweat-soaked five-, ten-, and fifty-rupee notes, pressing them into my hands with whispered prayers that brought tears to my eyes.
 
"May Allah bless your hands, Bibi," the blacksmith's widow told me, handing over forty rupees in rusted coins. "My son wrote his first letter to his uncle in the army yesterday. You have given my house eyes to see the world."
 
Those crumpled notes held no taint of patriarchal charity; they were not the condescending handouts of an angry father or the transactional bribe of a violent brother. They were the holy currency of honest labor, earned through knowledge, blessed by the tears of the poor.
 
I was no longer a beggar in my father's house; I was an independent woman building the foundation of her own empire.
