### Author's Note & Preface: Why This Book Exists
I began writing this book on a freezing night in February 2024, sitting on an iron cot in Room 42 of Amna Hall at Bahauddin Zakariya University in Multan.

My hands were shaking from the lingering effects of an undiagnosed neurological breakdown: a physical collapse that forced me to take a one-year academic drop and spend months lying on rusted iron beds in Nishtar Hospital, listening to patriarchal doctors dismiss two years of systemic institutional exhaustion as female hysteria.

For two years, like thousands of young women across Pakistan, I had practiced the exhausting, soul-crushing art of reflexive agreement. I had nodded to corrupt hostel wardens who stole our food and maintenance budgets. I had nodded to arrogant professors who threatened our degrees if we questioned outdated dogmas. I had nodded to family elders who believed that a girl's only virtue is her silence.

I nodded until my body revolted.

The human body is not built to carry the burden of continuous lying. When your mouth is forbidden to speak the truth, your nervous system mounts a violent biological strike.

This book is the record of what happened when the nodding stopped.

It chronicles two years inside Amna Hall, our battles over sulfur bore water, food cartels, curfew registers, and campus tear gas. It documents how seven girls in a twelve-by-fourteen room formed an unbreakable garrison of mutual defense, how we rescued our sister Sidra from a forced marriage using an artist case and a Metro bus ticket, and how we rejected the commercial 5,000-rupee plagiarized thesis mills to produce an internationally indexed empirical study on sovereign circular debt.

As of 2026, I remain a student at Bahauddin Zakariya University. But I no longer walk Bosan Road with lowered eyes.

Every chapter of this volume concludes with **The Strategist's Margin: Tactical Protocols for Daring Women**: the operational rules of power, institutional game theory, legal self-defense, and psychological boundary enforcement that every young woman needs to survive and dominate behind institutional enemy lines.

Do not let them buy your peace with your own silence.

*Anam Sajjad* 
*Multan, February 2026*

# Prologue: The Cost of the Reflex

The small seminar room on the third floor of the Department of Political Science at Bahauddin Zakariya University had three ceiling fans. Two of them turned with a rhythmic, metal scrape that sounded like an old pair of garden shears cutting through wire. The third fan did not turn at all. It hung motionless from the damp ceiling, its iron blades coated in the thick grey dust that settles over Multan every summer, like ash from an invisible volcano.

It was eleven in the morning in mid-August. Outside, the heat on Bosan Road was already touching forty-five degrees Celsius. The asphalt on the boulevard was softening into sticky tar that stuck to the soles of cheap plastic sandals. Inside Seminar Room 4, thirty-eight students sat on wooden benches designed for twenty-five. The air was heavy with the smell of sweat, damp notebooks, and the cheap black hair oil that the boys bought from the general store outside Chungi No. 9.

On the peeling plaster wall behind the podium hung three portraits in faded wooden frames: Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Allama Iqbal, and a former vice chancellor who had been removed in 2011 after an audit discovered eighty ghost employees on the campus maintenance payroll. The glass over the vice chancellor's portrait was cracked, a jagged line running from his forehead down to his neck tie.

At the wooden podium stood Dr. Qureshi. He was a man of sixty-two with yellowed teeth, an untrimmed white beard, and a grey safari suit that he wore regardless of whether the temperature was ten degrees or fifty. For forty minutes, he had been explaining the concept of social contract theory. He did not use books. He did not cite recent journals. He used a bundle of dog-eared, yellowing foolscap sheets that had clearly been photocopied twenty years earlier from an Indian textbook long out of print.

"In an ordered society," Dr. Qureshi said, tapping his chalk against the edge of the wood, "the individual surrenders their unruly personal judgment to the sovereign. Order requires compliance. When the citizen questions the ruler without sanction, the moral fabric of the state unravels. The citizen exists to serve the continuity of the state, not to disrupt it with insolent curiosity. Does everyone understand this fundamental truth?"

He stopped speaking. His small, watery eyes swept across the crowded wooden benches, daring a single eye to blink.

Every single head in the room moved in unison.

Up, then down. A smooth, rhythmic, collective dip of thirty-eight chins.

I felt the movement before I even consciously registered the thought in my brain. My chin dropped toward my chest, my neck bent, and my face arranged itself into that universal Pakistani expression of docile comprehension. A polite, submissive smile. A soft, deferential nod.

Yes, sir. Of course, sir. We agree, sir.

Sitting next to me on the wooden bench was Aqsa. Her knuckles were white against her blue plastic pencil box. She knew, and I knew, that the syllabus we had been reading for the past three weeks contradicted every single sentence Dr. Qureshi had just uttered. John Locke had argued the exact opposite in his Second Treatise of Civil Government. Jean-Jacques Rousseau had argued that sovereignty belongs inviolably to the people, and when authority breaches trust, obedience is not merely void, it becomes an act of cowardice. In our own country, the uncritical surrender of citizens to arbitrary rulers had produced decades of constitutional disasters, unbridled economic monopolies, circular debt, and institutional decay.

Aqsa's lips parted. She leaned forward half an inch. She was going to speak.

Under the wooden bench, my hand reached out and gripped her wrist. I squeezed hard, my fingernails pressing into the fabric of her sleeve.

"Do not," I whispered, barely moving my lips, keeping my eyes glued to the open margins of my notebook. "Do not do it, Aqsa. Just nod."

She turned her head to look at me. Her brown eyes were furious, bright with that dangerous, burning clarity that gets young girls thrown out of university departments. But she felt the pressure of my fingers on her pulse. She looked at Dr. Qureshi, whose chalk was poised like a dagger over his attendance register. If he wrote a red cross next to a student's name, their internal assessment marks would vanish into thin air. If he decided a student was insolent, no appeal to the Dean could restore their grade.

Aqsa swallowed. Her shoulder dropped. Slowly, reluctantly, her chin tilted downward.

She nodded.

Dr. Qureshi smiled, a satisfied, benevolent curl of his dry lips. "Good," he said, turning back to the blackboard. "At least this batch has basic manners. In politics, manners are more useful than cleverness."

Under the bench, I let go of Aqsa's wrist. My palm was damp with cold sweat.

My neck ached. It was not a physical cramp from sitting on a hard wooden bench. It was an ache deeper down, inside the tendon, in the small hinge where the base of the skull meets the spine.

That hinge is where women in Pakistan are trained to carry the weight of survival.

From the day a girl is born in our cities and towns, she is taught that agreement is the highest female virtue. When your father decides which college you will attend, you nod. When your mother tells you to lower your voice because your uncle has walked into the veranda, you nod. When your brother takes the larger piece of chicken and the cooler room near the fan, you nod. When your cousins lecture you on modesty while staring at your collarbone, you pull your chadar tighter around your shoulders, you look down at your slippers, and you nod.

You are told that nodding keeps you safe. You are told that nodding proves your tarbiyat, your family honor, your sweet nature.

They do not tell you the truth.

The truth is that nodding is a slow, silent poison. Every time you bow your head to a statement you know is a lie, a small piece of your intellect rots. Every time you agree to an arbitrary rule designed to demean your dignity, you sign an invisible contract that makes you smaller, quieter, and more convenient for the people who profit from your silence.

The habit of nodding is not an accident of culture. It is an engineered institutional reflex.

Consider what happens inside a Pakistani university department when a young woman refuses to nod. If she raises her hand to question a factual error in a lecture, she is labeled argumentative. If she questions why the departmental library closes at two in the afternoon while the boys' common room stays open until nine, she is summoned to the chairperson's office and questioned about her family background. If she refuses to accept an arbitrary grade reduction from a lecturer whose advances she ignored, her character is dissected in the faculty smoking lounge before the semester ends.

The institution does not need to use physical violence to enforce discipline. It relies on a far more insidious weapon: the threat of social ruin.

In Multan, where everyone knows everyone's maternal grandfather, a rumor travels faster than the dust on Bosan Road. A single sentence dropped by a department clerk into the ear of a visiting uncle can end a girl's educational career in twenty-four hours.

"Sajjad sahib's daughter has become very bold in the city," the uncle will say over evening tea. "She was seen arguing with a professor. She does not lower her gaze. A girl with that much tongue will bring trouble to your doorstep."

To protect our families from that whispering campaign, we nod. We nod until our necks lose the ability to hold our heads upright. We nod until we begin to believe our own silence.

For two long years inside the walls of Amna Hall at Bahauddin Zakariya University, I nodded. I nodded to corrupt wardens who stole our food budget to buy imported lawn suits for their daughters. I nodded to arrogant professors who threatened our marks if we asked for primary data instead of photocopied lecture slides. I nodded to doctors at Nishtar Hospital who told me that my agonizing, undiagnosed nerve pain was just female nervousness. I nodded to distant relatives who whispered that a girl from a respectable family should finish her degree quickly, pack her tin trunk, and go to her husband's house without opening her mouth.

I nodded until my body collapsed. I nodded until my nervous system broke down. I nodded until I lost an entire academic year to an illness that no hospital in Multan could name, an illness born entirely from the violent friction between what my soul knew and what my mouth was forbidden to say.

This book is the record of what happened when the nodding stopped.

It is not an academic paper, though it contains data that powerful people in our university tried very hard to bury. It is not a polite memoir, because politeness is the velvet glove that institutional bullies wear when they choke you.

It is a field guide for every Pakistani girl who has ever felt that suffocating knot in her throat while sitting on a wooden bench, watching someone with authority tell her that black is white, that corruption is culture, and that obedience is her only duty.

You do not owe them your agreement. You never did.

, -

The reflex of agreement does not begin in university lecture halls. It is cultivated in the quiet courtyards of our childhood homes long before we ever learn to read a constitutional preamble.

I remember my grandmother, Dadi Jan, sitting on a low wooden stool in our ancestral village near Shujabad, peeling garlic cloves into an aluminum dish. She had lived through the partition of 1947, through the wars of 1965 and 1971, through the martial laws and the economic droughts that periodically burned southern Punjab. She had raised six sons and three daughters on the pension of an irrigation canal supervisor.

One evening, when I was ten years old, my elder brother threw a brass dinner plate across the brick courtyard because the lentils had too much salt. My mother hurried to pick up the spilled lentils, wiping the floor with her cotton dupatta, her eyes lowered, whispering apologies. My father did not reprimand my brother; he simply turned the volume of the radio higher.

I stood by the hand-pump, trembling with indignation.

"Why is mother apologizing to him?" I asked Dadi Jan, my small hands clenched into fists. "He broke the plate! He shouted at her! She did not put too much salt; the salt bag got damp from the rain!"

My grandmother reached out, caught my wrist in her dry, papery fingers, and pulled me down beside her stool. She dipped a peeled garlic clove into water.

"Listen to me, Anam," the old woman whispered, her voice smelling of crushed cloves and snuff. "A girl who argues in the courtyard is like a glass jug on an iron cart. She will shatter at the first pothole on the road. When the men of the house are angry, you do not explain. You do not correct their memory. You lower your chin, you look at the floor tiles, and you say: 'Ji, it was my fault.' It costs you nothing to move your head up and down. A nod buys you twenty-four hours of peace."

"Dadi," I persisted, "but it was not her fault!"

"Truth does not matter in a kitchen, child," Dadi Jan sighed, closing her eyes. "Peace matters. A woman who keeps her neck soft will die in her own bed with her marriage shroud intact. A woman who stiffens her neck will find the whole world reaching for a stick to break it."

She believed she was saving me. She loved me with an ancient, desperate tenderness, and she was passing down the only defensive strategy that had kept three generations of peasant women alive under feudal masters.

She did not foresee that the twenty-four hours of peace bought by a submissive nod is an illusion.

The peace is never permanent. It is merely a temporary ceasefire purchased by surrendering a piece of your sovereign territory. Each time you nod to an injustice, the oppressor does not become grateful; he becomes bolder. He realizes that your boundaries are flexible, your dignity is negotiable, and your silence can be purchased for the low price of avoiding an uncomfortable argument.

By the time I reached Bahauddin Zakariya University, that childhood lesson had calcified into a second nervous system.

When Dr. Qureshi lectured on social contract theory in Seminar Room 4, quoting outdated interpretations that reduced citizens to serfs, the entire room complied because every girl on those wooden benches had a grandmother like Dadi Jan. We had all been trained in the domestic school of preemptive surrender. We had all been taught that the safest thing a woman can do with her mind is to hide it inside her chest until everyone with authority leaves the room.

It was this realization that made my throat tighten as I sat beside Aqsa.

We were not just students listening to a bad lecture on political philosophy. We were the custodians of our own subjugation. If we continued to nod, we would graduate, we would enter the civil secretariat, the schools, the banks, and the drawing rooms of this country, and we would teach the exact same cowardice to the next generation of daughters.

The chain had to be broken somewhere. And it had to begin with the small, terrifying decision to keep my chin level.

### The Strategist's Margin: Tactical Protocols for Daring Women

#### Protocol 1: The Rule of the Three-Second Pause

1. **Break the Muscle Memory of Agreement:** The human neck has a subconscious reflex. When confronted with an authoritative assertion, most young women nod within half a second to relieve immediate social tension. From this day forward, introduce a conscious three-second freeze. When a professor, family elder, or supervisor makes a dogmatic assertion, do not move your chin. Keep your eyes steady, your spine straight, and count three silent beats in your chest: one, two, three. That small pause completely disrupts the social expectation of instant submission. It signals to the speaker that their words are being weighed, not swallowed whole.
2. **Distinguish Civility from Intellectual Surrender:** You can be exceptionally polite without endorsing an intellectual falsehood. A calm, neutral statement such as, "I hear your perspective, sir," or "That is a well-known interpretation of the doctrine," commits you to nothing. It leaves your intellectual dignity entirely intact while depriving the institutional bully of any pretext to accuse you of disrespect or insolence.
3. **The Nightly Audit of Concessions:** Every evening before you sleep, ask yourself one ruthless question: What lie did I endorse today simply because I was afraid of the awkwardness of silence? Did I agree with a sexist joke? Did I validate a corrupt procedure? Did I pretend an unfair grade was acceptable? Write it down in a private journal. What is named can be observed, and what is observed can eventually be dismantled.
4. **The Principle of Neutral Eye Contact:** In conservative institutional spaces, looking down is read as guilt or submission; looking up aggressively is read as defiance. The golden path is neutral horizontal gaze. Look directly between the speaker's eyebrows. It prevents them from reading emotional distress in your pupils while projecting absolute, immovable self-possession.

5. **The De-Escalation of Manufactured Moral Crises:** In patriarchal systems, an authority figure who is losing an intellectual argument will almost always pivot from academic substance to personal decorum. They will say: "You have an attitude problem," "You do not know how to speak to your elders," or "Where is your modesty?" Recognize this maneuver as an intellectual concession. The speaker has abandoned the facts because the facts do not support them. Respond with absolute emotional flatness: "I am addressing the historical data, sir. My respect for your office is intact, but the regulatory record remains what it is."
6. **The Power of the Strategic Silence:** You are never required to fill an awkward silence created by another person's aggression. When a supervisor or professor attempts to bully you into agreement by staring at you after a dogmatic statement, simply look back with neutral composure. Human beings have an intense psychological aversion to silence; weak people rush to agree just to terminate the quiet. Let the silence hang. The person who breaks the silence is usually the person who surrenders their ground.

# Chapter 1: The February Midnight (Room 42)

The cold in Multan during February does not behave like the cold in northern towns. It does not come with clean mountain breezes or crisp, white snow. It comes as a thick, yellow-grey fog that rolls off the agricultural fields around Bosan Road, saturating the ancient brickwork with damp, bone-chilling mist.

The fog carries the smell of burning cow dung, sugarcane bagasse from the local jaggery pans, and the sulfurous exhaust of overloaded diesel trucks that idle all night on the bypass. By midnight, the moisture hangs so heavy in the air that the streetlamps along the campus road look like drowning yellow lanterns, their halos bleeding into the dark.

It was two in the morning on the tenth of February.

Inside Room 42 of Amna Hall, the concrete walls felt like the inside of a cold storage locker. The university had turned off the main gas line at eleven at night, claiming winter fuel conservation, though everyone in the hostel knew that the superintendent's private bungalow across the eastern lawn had two radiant gas heaters burning until dawn.

I was sitting on the edge of my iron cot, wrapped in three layers of clothing: a thick woolen sweater my mother had knitted five years earlier from unravelled grey yarn, an oversized fleece jacket, and a heavy brown handloom shawl pulled tight over my ears like a monk's cowl. My feet were shoved into double wool socks inside rubber bathroom slippers, yet my toes were completely numb, white and stiff as tallow candles.

On the concrete floor between my cot and Sidra's bed sat a blue plastic bucket. Inside the bucket, a contraband immersion rod hummed softly.

The copper coil was black with years of built-up mineral scale from Multan's saline bore water. It hissed like a cornered snake as it heated three inches of water in a dented steel mug. We were strictly forbidden to possess immersion rods. If the hostel supervisor discovered an electrical heating element during a corridor raid, the penalty was five thousand rupees on your semester fee voucher, plus a mandatory disciplinary notice mailed to your father's village address.

Yet without that red-hot copper coil, we would have frozen to death. In Room 42, survival was a series of small, calculated violations of university bylaws.

I reached out and touched the steel mug with the back of my fingernail. It was hot. I dropped in a spoonful of cheap Danedar tea dust, half a packet of powdered milk, and a dark lump of unrefined brown sugar that Iqra had smuggled from her family's sugarcane farm near Lodhran.

The steam rose in a thin, fragrant curl into the freezing air. It was the only warmth in the entire room.

Sidra was asleep on the cot next to mine, breathing with that shallow, exhausted rhythm of someone whose dreams are troubled. Her face was half-hidden under a heavy cotton quilt that had been mended at the seams with yellow twine. Next to her pillow lay a folded white envelope from her village near Khanewal. Her paternal uncle had finalized a wedding date for June, to a cousin twelve years older than her who ran a fertilizer depot near the railway crossing.

She had spent three hours crying on the washroom floor at nine in the evening, her forehead pressed against the cold ceramic tiles, begging God to let her complete her master's degree before her uncle signed away her life. Her eyes had swollen shut with weeping, and then she had fallen into a heavy, defeated stupor that looked more like an anesthetic coma than natural sleep.

Across the narrow room, on the upper bunk of the opposite cot, Saba was reading by the glow of a rechargeable Chinese torch. She held the plastic cylinder between her teeth, turning the yellowed pages of an Indian edition of international political economy with gloved fingers. Her breath made small, rhythmic white puffs in the darkness.

I turned back to my wooden study table.

The surface of the desk was scarred with old ink stains, ring marks from countless tea cups, and names carved by girls who had lived in Room 42 over the past thirty years. Near the edge of the wood was a carved inscription from 1998: *Farzana, Roll 104, Don't give up.* I wondered where Farzana was now. Was she teaching in a college? Was she trapped in a courtyard in Dera Ghazi Khan, stirring a pot of lentils while her husband counted her phone calls?

In the corner of the desk lay my medical file from Nishtar Hospital.

It was three inches thick, held together by a fraying green ribbon. Inside were dozens of laboratory reports: complete blood pictures, erythrocyte sedimentation rates, rheumatoid factors, thyroid panels, brain CT scans, and nerve conduction studies. Every single test carried the same printed conclusion at the bottom: *Within Normal Limits.*

Yet for twelve agonizing months, I had been unable to lift my arms without blinding, electric pain shooting down my cervical spine into my fingertips. My hands had developed involuntary tremors that made my teacup rattle against the saucer. Some mornings, I would step onto the cold concrete floor of Room 42 and my legs would simply give way, dropping me to my knees in front of my iron trunk while my roommates rushed to catch my arms.

The chief physician at Nishtar Hospital, a heavy man with gold cufflinks, an expensive watch, and an impatient scowl, had spent less than four minutes looking at my file. He had tapped the laboratory sheets against his mahogany desk, adjusted his gold-rimmed spectacles, and said to my father:

"The girl has nothing physical, Sajjad sahib. All her organs are healthy. It is functional tension. Female hysteria caused by the stress of university examinations. Young women from small towns often panic when confronted with the city. Give her these multivitamins, take her home, and arrange her marriage. A proper domestic routine and children will settle her nerves."

My father had sat there on the vinyl hospital chair, his weathered hands clutching his wooden prayer beads, nodding respectfully to the doctor.

"Ji-achha, doctor sahib. Thank you, doctor sahib. You know best."

And I, sitting on the cold stainless steel examination stool, with my fingers trembling in my lap and tears of humiliation burning my eyes, had lowered my head and nodded too.

Yes, doctor. Of course, doctor. I am just a nervous girl.

That nod had cost me an entire year of my life.

Because I bowed to that arrogant doctor's diagnosis, my father had filed a formal application to freeze my academic session. A one-year drop. In Pakistan, when a young man takes an extra year, people say he is preparing for the Central Superior Services examinations or exploring entrepreneurial ventures. When a female student drops an academic year, the neighborhood talks in poisonous, hushed whispers.

Did she have a scandal? Did she meet a boy on Bosan Road? Is she mentally unstable? Is she damaged goods?

I had spent eight long months locked inside my family's three-room house in our small district town, staring at the whitewashed ceiling, listening to the ceiling fan cut through the suffocating summer silence. My body was broken, but it was not broken by a bacterial infection or a viral fever.

It was broken by twenty years of swallowed truth.

Every unsaid objection, every forced smile, every swallowed insult from corrupt administrators, entitled lecturers, and patriarchal elders had turned inward, transforming into an autoimmune rage that attacked my own nervous system. The human body is not built to carry the burden of continuous lying. When your mind refuses to scream, your nerves scream for you.

It was on that freezing February night in Room 42, watching the steam rise from that dented steel mug, that something inside my chest hardened into unbreakable iron.

I opened a fresh, unlined spiral notebook that I had bought from the stationery shop near the department for two hundred rupees. The paper was rough, off-white, and smelled faintly of wood pulp and damp ink.

My fingers were trembling, partly from the biting cold, partly from the residual nerve damage in my wrists.

I picked up a blue ballpoint pen. I rested my forearm on the scarred wood of the desk.

I wrote the first sentence:

*I have nodded my life away, and the bill has arrived in my bones.*

I stared at those words for five long minutes. The ink looked dark, permanent, and terrifying against the blank page.

Saba turned a page in her book on the upper bunk. The paper rustled loudly in the still room.

"Anam?" she whispered, taking the plastic torch from her mouth. "Why are you still awake? The temperature is dropping below four degrees. Turn off that immersion rod and come under the quilt."

"I am writing, Saba," I said softly, not looking back.

"Writing what? The notes for Comparative Government?"

"No," I replied, tracing the wet ink with my thumb. "A record. A record of everything they told us to swallow, and what it costs to digest it."

Saba was quiet for a long moment. Then I heard the mattress creak as she shifted her weight. She pulled her heavy blanket tighter around her shoulders and rested her chin on the wooden guardrail of the bunk, looking down at me in the dim yellow light.

"Be careful what you put on paper, Anam," she said, her voice dropping to a low, urgent murmur. "Paper has ears in Amna Hall. If the warden finds out you are keeping an investigative journal about what happens behind these green gates, she will confiscate it before you can write the final chapter. She will call your father. She will tell him that his daughter has become an ideological rebel."

"Let her try," I whispered, dipping my fingertips near the hot steel mug to warm them. "A warden can seize a cell phone. She can lock an iron gate at five-thirty. But she cannot seize what a woman writes when she has already lost an entire year of her life. I have already died once in that hospital room. They have nothing left to threaten me with."

That night, between two and five in the morning, while the yellow fog pressed against the barred windowpanes and the cold crept through every crack in the brickwork, I wrote eighteen pages.

I did not write with polite academic detachment. I did not use the passive voice that professors love because it allows them to hide human guilt behind institutional jargon. I wrote names. I wrote dates. I wrote about the five-hundred-rupee bribes demanded by clerks for processing clean gate passes. I wrote about the saline bore water that made our scalps bleed. I wrote about the girl from Muzaffargarh who was expelled because someone saw her talking to her male cousin outside the Metro station.

By the time the call to prayer drifted across Bosan Road from the university central mosque, my steel mug was cold. The immersion rod sat silent in the empty blue bucket.

My fingers were stiff, but the tremor in my right wrist had stopped completely.

For the first time in eighteen months, the burning ache in my cervical spine was gone.

The body heals the moment you stop lying to it.

, -

The fog outside Room 42 grew thicker as the night deepened, pressing against the windowpanes like a living, yellow beast trying to find a crack in the brickwork.

From the courtyard below, the muffled bark of a stray dog echoed off the concrete fortress of Amna Hall, sharp, hollow, and lonely.

I rubbed my stiff hands together over the steel mug. The tea was lukewarm now, coated with a thin, pale skin of boiled milk powder. The immersion rod sat cooling on the rim of the blue bucket, its black copper coils giving off the sharp, metallic smell of baked river silt.

I looked at the three notebooks stacked on the corner of my wooden desk.

The cooler roared. The fan whipped the air into a frenzy. Anza stood directly beneath the spinning blades, lifting her dripping face to the wind, panting like an athlete at the end of a marathon.

"That girl is an emotional terrorist!" Anza declared with venomous passion, wiping sweat from her chin. "She has no mercy in her soul! Na khud sukoon karti hai, na humein karne deti hai! If she ever turns off our fan again in forty-degree heat, Anam, I swear I will bolt this door from the inside and let her weep in the hallway until the sun comes up!"

I fell back onto my cot, laughing despite my exhaustion and aching back. In Amna Hall, survival was not just about enduring the university administration; it was about absorbing the collateral emotional damage of our sisters' shattered hearts.



Amna Hall did not look like a place of higher learning. It looked like a medium-security correctional facility built by people who deeply distrusted the concept of female literacy.

The boundary wall was twelve feet high, constructed from rough red bricks capped with two feet of rusted concertina razor wire. At the top of the wall, shards of broken brown glass bottles had been embedded into wet cement forty years ago, their jagged edges pointing toward the sky to deter anyone foolish enough to attempt an escape. The red brick was stained with black mold along the water pipes and covered in faded chalk slogans from previous student union campaigns: *Haq-o-Insaaf, Inqilab, Talba Rabita Council.*

The main entrance was a massive double gate made of heavy sheet iron, painted a sickly shade of dark green that had blistered and peeled under decades of southern Punjab sun.

Set into the right side of the massive iron gate was a smaller, narrow wicket door, barely four feet high and eighteen inches wide. To enter Amna Hall, every resident had to bend their spine, lower their head, and step sideways over a sharp steel sill while dragging their belongings behind them.

The architecture itself forced you to bow before you took a single step into the compound. It was a physical rehearsal for the psychological obedience demanded inside.

Inside the wicket door sat Maasi Sakina.

She was an institution older than the university itself. A woman of indeterminate age, somewhere between fifty-five and seventy, with skin like dried leather, missing molars, and a permanent wad of chewing tobacco tucked into her lower lip. She sat on a low wooden charpai covered in a faded ralli quilt, positioned so that her gaze intercepted every single person who crossed the threshold. Her bare feet were cracked at the heels, grey with brick dust, and she wore a silver amulet wrapped in black thread around her withered ankle.

Next to her charpai was a heavy wooden table on which rested the Master Register: a massive, canvas-bound ledger with water-stained blue paper.

"Stop right there," Maasi Sakina rasped, spitting a stream of dark red tobacco juice into a rusted tin can behind her bed. "Father stays outside. No males past the green door. Not your father, not your brother, not your grandfather. Leave the trunk on the gravel."

My father stood outside the wicket door, peering nervously through the iron bars like a man visiting an inmate.

"Maasi ji," he said politely, holding out my allotment paper with both hands. "She is new. Room 42. Can I carry her trunk to the veranda? It is very heavy. Her hands will blister."

"Rules are rules, babu," she replied without looking up from her ledger. "If I let your feet touch this gravel, the warden will deduct five hundred rupees from my salary at the end of the month. The girl has two arms. Let her pull it. If she cannot pull her own trunk, she should stay home and stir the lentil pot."

My father looked at me, his eyes full of that helpless, aching apology that poor men give their daughters when they hit the brick wall of petty authority.

"It is alright, Baba," I said, forcing a steady voice. "I can pull it. You go back to the Metro station before the heat gets worse. Go home."

He hesitated. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a crumpled five-hundred-rupee note, folded into a tiny triangle. He pressed it into my palm, closing my fingers over it.

"Keep it inside your Quran," he whispered. "Do not show it to anyone. Call me from the PCO booth on Sunday afternoon. Recite Ayat-ul-Kursi before you sleep."

He turned and walked away down the dusty asphalt path, his shoulders hunched, his worn leather shoes kicking up small puffs of red dust. I stood at the threshold, watching his grey figure shrink until he disappeared behind the eucalyptus trees near the central library.

I turned back to the wicket door.

The green trunk weighed thirty kilograms. The gravel path was rough, filled with broken brick chips and sharp stones. I gripped the iron handle with both hands, bent my knees, and hauled it across the concrete sill. The metal bottom scraped against the stone with a terrible, screeching sound that echoed through the courtyard.

Maasi Sakina watched me with cold, indifferent eyes.

"Name?" she asked, dipping a rusted steel nib into an inkpot.

"Anam Sajjad."

"Department?"

"Political Science."

She wrote slowly, her gnarled fingers gripping the wooden penholder like an iron wrench.

"Room 42. East Wing. Third floor. Top of the stairs, turn left, last door on the right. You will share with five girls. If you lose your room key, the duplicate costs two hundred rupees. Curfew is five-thirty in the evening. At five-thirty-one, the iron bar falls across this gate. If you are outside when the bar falls, you sleep on the concrete outside until the morning warden arrives at eight."

"What if our afternoon lecture finishes at five in the department?" I asked.

She stopped writing. Slowly, deliberately, she lifted her head. Her yellowed eyes narrowed as she looked at me, as if an ant had suddenly spoken in fluent Urdu.

"Listen to me, girl," she said, her voice dropping to a harsh whisper. "You have just entered through that door. Do not bring your mouth in with you. In Amna Hall, the clock belongs to the warden, not to your department. If your professor keeps you until five-fifteen, you run. If you cannot run, you drop the lecture. But if that iron bar falls while you are on Bosan Road, your name goes into the Red Book. Three entries in the Red Book, and your father receives a registered letter stating that his daughter is wandering the streets of Multan after dark. Do you want that letter to reach your village?"

My stomach turned to ice.

A letter like that in my village was not an administrative notice. It was a social death sentence. It was the kind of piece of paper that caused uncles to demand that a girl be locked inside the house and married off to the nearest available cousin before the family honor was stained.

"No, Maasi," I said softly.

"Then pull your trunk and climb the stairs," she said, spitting into her tin can again.

I dragged the trunk across the gravel.

The courtyard of Amna Hall was a vast, bleak square of hard-packed earth where nothing grew except three stunted neem trees covered in white chemical dust from the nearby construction work. The four-story hostel building surrounded the square like the walls of a fortress. Every window on the first three floors was barred with thick criss-cross iron grates. On the fourth floor, the balconies were covered in corrugated fiberglass sheets that trapped the rising heat and turned the corridors into ovens.

Lines of cheap, faded plastic clotheslines criss-crossed the space between the pillars, sagging under the weight of wet dupattas, patched shalwars, and grey towels that smelled of borewell sulfur.

As I dragged my trunk toward the dark stairwell of the East Wing, I heard the sounds of two hundred female lives compressed into concrete boxes:

The hiss of illegal immersion rods boiling water in plastic mugs.

The rhythmic, dull thumping of wooden washing paddles beating wet clothes against concrete slabs in the communal washrooms.

A transistor radio playing an old Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan qawwali from a second-floor window, its batteries dying, the voice warping in the heavy heat.

And from somewhere down the corridor, the quiet, muffled sound of a young girl crying into her pillow, so soft that if you were not listening for it, you would think it was just the wind whistling through the razor wire on the roof.

I reached the bottom of the concrete staircase.

There were sixty-four steps to the third floor. The edges of the concrete steps were chipped and broken, exposing rusted iron reinforcement rods that stuck out like broken teeth.

I stood there, looking up into the dark stairwell. My hands were already blistered. My back throbbed with a dull, sickening ache.

I had thought that getting into the university on open merit, scoring ninety-two percent in my intermediate examinations, was the battle. I had thought that intellectual excellence was the coin that purchased your dignity in an institution of higher learning.

Standing at the bottom of those sixty-four concrete steps, looking up into the suffocating gloom of Amna Hall, I understood my first political science lesson:

Excellence means nothing inside an empire of petty gatekeepers. Here, the only currency was endurance, and the only shield was the secret alliance of the women who lived behind the wire.

Every twenty steps on the climb to the third floor, a narrow casement window opened onto the narrow alleyway behind Amna Hall. The windows were covered in rusted wire mesh so thick that you could barely see through to the outside world, designed to ensure that no resident could ever drop a handwritten note or catch a glimpse of the boys' university boulevard.

On the landing of the second floor, a group of four senior students in faded cotton kurtas were sitting on the concrete steps, sharing a single plastic plate of cold aloo samosas from the canteen.

One of them: a thin girl with sharp cheekbones and a purple dupatta pinned under her chin, watched me struggle with my green trunk.

When the July heatwave arrives, the concrete slabs on the roof turn into tandoor stones. You will sleep with a wet towel over your chest, and by three in the morning, the towel will be bone-dry and stiff as cardboard."

"Doesn't the administration install ceiling insulation?" I asked, panting against the wall.

The four girls looked at each other and burst into harsh, dry laughter.

"Look out the main gate next time you go to the Metro station," she said, leaning against the damp brick wall. "The Chief Warden's private bungalow on the bypass got Italian marble flooring, three five-ton inverter air conditioners, and a landscaped lawn with imported hybrid roses. We got two new plastic buckets per floor and a sermon in the common room about the virtue of female patience during economic hardship."

She picked up the last piece of samosa crust, popped it into her mouth, and wiped her fingers on her shalwar.

"Welcome to Amna Hall, fresher. If you want to survive here, remember the three golden rules: boil your water twice, hide your immersion rod inside your pillowcase during raids, and never, ever tell Maasi Sakina what time your afternoon classes actually finish."

I hauled the green trunk up the remaining twenty steps to the third floor.

The corridor stretched seventy yards from east to west: a long, dim tunnel of raw concrete, illuminated only by four bare bulbs hanging from frayed ceiling cords. Every eight feet, an identical green wooden door opened onto a room.

Outside each door sat a row of dusty rubber bathroom slippers, plastic water bottles filled with yellow saline water, and cardboard cartons used as makeshift shoe racks.

From behind the closed doors drifted the collective, intimate soundtrack of female survival:

The click-clack of a mechanical typewriter being used by an Urdu literature student working on a research term paper.

The rhythmic, bubbling hiss of water boiling in an aluminum kettle over an illegal electric coil.

A girl's voice reciting verses of the Holy Quran in a soft, melodic, mournful cadence that vibrated through the thin brick partition.

And down at the far end of the hall, near the communal washrooms, the loud, angry shouting of two roommates fighting over an empty plastic bucket:

"I carried this bucket from the Metro filtration plant on my own shoulders, Naila! You did not carry a single liter! Wash your clothes with the khara paani or wait until Sunday!"

I stood in the middle of that dim, suffocating corridor, my hands raw and bleeding from the iron handle of my trunk.

In our small district town, my father's house had three rooms and a courtyard with a pomegranate tree. It was poor, but it was quiet. The air smelled of clean morning soil, woodsmoke, and the mint leaves my mother grew in cracked clay pots. When the sun went down, the only sound was the wind rustling through the wheat fields and the distant call to prayer from the village mosque.

Here, inside Amna Hall, there was no silence.

There was only the dense, frantic, compressed friction of two hundred young women fighting for an inch of space, a liter of clean water, and a breath of cool air, governed by an administration that viewed our presence as an annoying logistical burden to be minimized at the lowest possible cost.

I walked down the concrete floor, reading the chipped white numbers painted on the green doors:

Room 28. Room 30. Room 32.

And finally, at the very end of the corridor, directly opposite the dark, damp opening of the communal washrooms:

**Room 42.**

The door was unlatched, standing open half an inch.

I placed my palm against the rough, splintered wood, took a deep breath of the sulfur-tainted air, and pushed it open.

### The Strategist's Margin: Tactical Protocols for Daring Women

#### Protocol 4: Decoding Institutional Panopticons (Operating Under Surveillance)

1. **Map the Informal Hierarchy Immediately:** In every closed institution, the real power rarely resides in the person with the official title. The hostel superintendent signs the forms, but the gatekeeper, the sweeper, and the mess accountant control your daily reality. Identify who holds the keys, who logs the hours, and who handles the petty paperwork. Never fight a battle with a low-level enforcer; neutralize them through predictable compliance in public while building your independence in private.
2. **The Weaponization of Moral Terror:** Notice how administrative control in conservative societies is almost always framed as moral protection. When an institution threatens your reputation rather than your academic standing, recognize it as an asymmetric intimidation tactic. Protect your flank: ensure all your official communications, gate passes, and lecture timetables are stamped, photographed, and filed before you leave any department.
3. **Physical Territory and Mental Sanity:** In high-density, shared institutional spaces, claim a physical perimeter immediately. Even if it is only a single wooden shelf or the space under your iron cot, establish absolute order over that space. Psychological sovereignty begins with the neatness of your immediate physical boundary.
4. **The Principle of Silent Passage:** When entering or leaving an enclosure governed by petty gatekeepers, minimize all emotional display. Do not appear anxious; do not appear arrogant. Move with smooth, neutral efficiency. When you become memorable to an enforcer, you invite scrutiny; when you become routine, you gain operational invisibility.

5. **The Mapping of Institutional Corruption:** In underfunded public institutions, the systematic diversion of development funds (infrastructure grants, student welfare budgets, maintenance allocations) is ubiquitous. Never view infrastructure failures as accidental incompetence; treat them as evidence of structural extraction. Discreetly identify which senior administrators control the procurement committees, which contractors hold perpetual monopolies, and where external regulatory oversight can be triggered.
6. **The Protocol of Low-Profile Arrival:** When entering a new residential or operational facility, avoid conspicuous displays of wealth, high-end electronics, or family influence. Flashy arrivals invite predatory administrative taxation, peer resentment, and opportunistic theft. Blend seamlessly into the baseline environment. True power moves in quiet, unassuming utility clothing until its institutional perimeter is fully secured.

# Chapter 4: The Currency of Sifarish (Allotment)


The corridors of Amna Hall did not merely hold academic notes and examination panic; they held the secret, dangerous emotional entanglements of girls who were experiencing psychological freedom for the very first time in their lives.

Two of those entanglements shook our wing to its foundations.

The first was the story of **Aqsa and Ammar**.

Aqsa had arrived in Multan from a conservative, mud-brick basti on the agricultural periphery of Muzaffargarh. She was an extraordinarily beautiful girl, with large, sorrowful eyes, a gentle voice, and a deep, village-bred piety that made her stand apart from the urban students. Before she set foot on the campus bus, she had placed her hand on her father's shoulder and sworn a sacred oath: she would wear her black abaya every single day, she would keep her eyes fixed on the pavement, she would not engage in modern campus frivolity, and she would never, under any circumstance, allow a man to enter her heart.

For her first two semesters, Aqsa was an untouchable fortress of modesty. She walked straight from Amna Hall to the Political Science lecture halls, attended her classes with downcast eyes, and returned immediately to the hostel library.

Then came Ammar.

Ammar was a third-year classmate from a prominent family in Khanewal: confident, articulate, wearing tailored kurtas and driving a red motorcycle that he parked ostentatiously near the department veranda. For seven relentless months, Ammar pursued Aqsa with the single-minded focus of a hunter. He sent study guides through third parties. He sat two rows behind her in every seminar. He left polite notes in her library books. For seven months, Aqsa repelled every advance with stony, freezing silence, refusing even to look in his direction.

Then came the day of the public explosion.

During a crowded departmental symposium in the main auditorium, before sixty students and three junior lecturers, Ammar lost his patience with her silence. He stood up in the middle of the lecture hall, walked down the aisle, and staged a dramatic, impassioned declaration of his love. He declared to the entire department that Aqsa was his destined wife, that no other male student should dare look in her direction, and that his intentions were honorable and pure.

The hall was stunned into breathless silence. For a girl from a traditional Muzaffargarh basti, where a girl's name mentioned in public with a boy meant an instant family jirga, disownment, and immediate forced marriage, Ammar's public stunt was not romantic. It was a terrifying public humiliation.

Aqsa rose from her wooden chair like an enraged queen. With a voice that rang through the rafters like a whip, she tore Ammar's dignity to shreds before the entire faculty. She called him a cowardly, dishonorable boy who possessed neither the manners of a gentleman nor the fear of God. She demanded that the department disciplinary committee take notice of his harassment, packed her books with trembling hands, and walked out of the auditorium with her head held high.

Yet human psychology is a dark, contradictory labyrinth.

That public confrontation broke the emotional wall between them. Over the following weeks, Ammar sent desperate, tearful apologies through mutual acquaintances, swearing that his outburst had been driven by overwhelming devotion rather than disrespect. Slowly, insidiously, Aqsa's righteous fury turned into curiosity, and that curiosity softened into conversation. Within three months, the girl who had sworn never to look at a man had fallen deeply, completely, and helplessly in love with Ammar.

And that was when the trap snapped shut.

The moment Aqsa gave her heart, the power dynamic inverted completely. Ammar, having achieved his conquest, began to retreat. He became cold, distant, and erratic. When Aqsa, terrified of discovery and longing for legitimate honor, pleaded with him to send his mother with a formal marriage proposal to her father, Ammar laughed, calling her naive and claiming that they needed years to understand each other before discussing marriage.

Every ignored message, every cold dismissal plunged Aqsa into a vortex of acute physical panic, culminating in the midnight episodes where she would burst into Room 42, turn off our cooling fans in forty-degree heat, and weep in our laps until dawn.

The second entanglement was even more bizarre and tragic: **The Loyalty Test of Saba and Anza**.

Saba, who lived in the gallery adjacent to Room 42, had a close departmental classmate from Vehari. This classmate had been secretly dating a charismatic student politics activist, but she was plagued by constant doubts regarding his fidelity. Suspecting that her boyfriend was flirting with other girls on WhatsApp, the classmate hatched a clandestine surveillance plot and enlisted Saba's help.

Saba brought the boy's mobile number into Room 42 and handed it to Anza.

"Anza," Saba had whispered conspiratorially, closing our wooden door. "My friend needs a girl who is clever, witty, and completely immune to male charms to test her boyfriend. You text him from a new SIM card. Flirt with him slightly. See if he bites the bait or if he stays loyal to her. If he tries to arrange a secret meeting, we will take screenshots and hand them to my friend."

Anza, who considered herself an untouchable cynic regarding campus romances, accepted the assignment with theatrical confidence. She bought a new SIM card, created a fictional persona, and sent the boy an enigmatic, teasing greeting.

The boy took the bait within three minutes.

For the first forty-eight hours, it was a hilarious covert game inside Room 42. Anza would draft witty, sarcastic messages while Sara and I watched, documenting how eagerly the boy abandoned his declared loyalty to Saba's friend. But then, as the text conversations deepened over late nights, something catastrophic occurred.

The boy was genuinely brilliant, sharp-tongued, and possessing a dark, poetic charisma that pierced straight through Anza's intellectual defenses. Within ten days, the fake surveillance chat had transformed into a raw, intense, authentic emotional connection.

Anza, who had set out to catch a traitor, was caught in the trap herself.

She stopped showing us the messages. She began staying awake until three in the morning, her face illuminated by the pale blue light of her phone screen, smiling with an expression of tender, secret intoxication that terrified me. When she finally confessed the truth to me on our cot, she was shaking with violent, uncontrollable guilt.

"Anam, main kya karoon?" Anza had sobbed, burying her face into my chest while the rain poured down onto the tin roof outside. "Mera dil baahar nikalne lag gaya hai! I was supposed to expose him for my friend, and now I cannot breathe if he does not text me for an hour! I feel like a disgusting traitor, but I love him. Main kya karoon?"

In Room 42, I found myself sitting between two shattered worlds: on one cot sat Aqsa, sobbing because a boy was pulling away; on the other cot sat Anza, sobbing because she had fallen for a boy she was supposed to destroy.

And in the middle of them stood I: nodding, consoling, offering water, absorbing their hysteria, carrying the crushing, silent weight of their secrets while my own spine burned with unaddressed fever.



Room 42 measured fourteen feet by twelve feet.

The room had originally been built in 1982 to house two senior research scholars in botany. It had two built-in brick alcoves for reference books, two narrow wooden wardrobes without functional locks, and one double casement window that looked out onto the boundary wall and the razor wire.

By 2024, through the magical arithmetic of university hostel administrations, Room 42 had been allotted to six girls.

When I pushed open the warped wooden door, the room was already full.

Four iron cots had been pushed together in pairs to create two massive communal sleeping platforms. In the narrow space between the two platforms, barely two feet wide, sat six tin trunks stacked three high like metal shipping containers. The air smelled of damp linen, cheap rose talcum powder, and the sharp, sulfurous stink of saline bore water that had seeped through the washroom wall.

Sitting on the lower bunk nearest the window was a tall girl with fair skin, sharp features, dark circles under her eyes, and a blue chiffon dupatta pinned tightly around her head with three steel safety pins.

She was unpacking a cardboard carton of books. When she saw me standing in the doorway with my green trunk, she stopped her work.

"You must be the sixth," she said. Her voice was flat, neither welcoming nor hostile, just the tired voice of someone who has seen an eviction notice arrive in the mail.

"Anam," I said, catching my breath and wiping sweat from my forehead. "Anam Sajjad. Political Science."

"Aqsa," she replied, wiping dust from a thick copy of Mazhar-ul-Haq's political theory textbook. "English Literature. Third semester. That is your corner."

She pointed with her chin toward the upper bunk on the left.

The upper cot was made of rusted angle iron with a sagging base of interwoven steel strips. On top of the steel strips lay a two-inch mattress made of recycled cotton waste that smelled faintly of kerosene. There was no pillow. There was no ladder. To get onto the cot, you had to place your foot on the lid of a tin trunk, grab the iron rail of the window, and haul yourself upward by your upper arms.

"Who are the other four?" I asked, sliding my trunk into the remaining six inches of floor space near the door.

"You will meet them when the sun goes down," Aqsa said, sitting cross-legged on her mattress. "Anza is at the library. Saba is at the administrative block trying to get her fee concession approved for the fourth time. Sidra is at the dispensary trying to get Panadol for her toothache. And Momina is somewhere in the art department avoiding the warden."

She looked at my green trunk, then at my face.

"Did your father pay a bribe for this allotment?"

The question was so blunt that it felt like a slap.

"No," I stammered, my cheeks burning. "I got this room on merit. First list. Ninety-two percent in intermediate."

Aqsa let out a short, dry laugh that sounded like dry leaves scraping across a brick floor.

"Merit," she said, shaking her head. "Girl, do you know why you are on the third floor in Room 42? Look out that window."

I walked over to the casement window and looked down.

Directly below us, twenty feet from the hostel wall, was the main drainage ditch for the northern campus. It was an open, concrete-lined nullah six feet wide, filled with black, sluggish sludge that smelled of rotten eggs and sewage. Swarms of black mosquitoes hovered over the water in clouds so thick they looked like smoke rising from a wet fire.

"The ground floor and first floor rooms facing the lawn are reserved for the daughters of section officers, deputy commissioners, and the nieces of senior professors," Aqsa said, leaning against the brick alcove. "They have two girls to a room. They have functional ceiling fans. Some of them have smuggled split air conditioners with private electricity lines tapped directly into the department transformer. But if you arrive here on pure merit, with a father who carries a clerk's pension card, you get Room 42 over the open sewer."

She leaned forward, her dark eyes fixing mine with an intensity that made me want to step back.

"Do you know who lived in this bed before you?"

"No," I whispered.

"A girl named Rabia. Economics department. From Bahawalnagar. Brilliant girl. Wrote research papers in Urdu and English. Her father died in her second year. When the fee hike came last autumn, her semester voucher went from twenty-eight thousand to forty-six thousand rupees. She went to the Warden's office every morning for three weeks with an application for the Student Welfare Fund."

Aqsa paused. The ceiling fan overhead gave a violent, metallic shudder, wobbling on its iron hook before settling back into its droning scrape.

"What happened to her?" I asked.

"The Warden told her that the welfare fund was exhausted," Aqsa said quietly. "Then the Assistant Warden told her that if she was truly needy, she should visit the home of a certain member of the university syndicate in Cantt on Sunday evening to have her application personally attested."

My hands went cold.

"Rabia did not go," Aqsa continued. "She sat on that very cot where your mattress is lying. She packed her tin trunk. She walked out through that green iron gate, took the Metro bus to the terminal, and went back to Bahawalnagar. She is married now to a cousin who runs a tire repair shop. She doesn't read books anymore."

Aqsa picked up her textbook and opened it to page one hundred and forty.

"That is what merit means inside these walls, Anam. Merit is what they make you believe in so you work sixteen hours a day to keep the university's ranking alive. But when the vouchers are printed, the only language this administration speaks is sifarish, money, or silence."

I sat down on the edge of my green trunk.

My hands rested on my knees. I looked up at the rusted angle iron of the bunk bed above me.

In my village, my uncles and neighbors had congratulated my father with garlands of yellow marigolds when the admission list was pinned to the board. They had said, *Sajjad sahib, your daughter is going to the university. She will become an officer. She will sit in an air-conditioned room with files.*

They did not know that the university was not an ivory tower. It was a market stall where the weak were traded for political peace, where every privilege had a price tag, and where a girl without a patron was expected to pay with her silence or her future.

I reached out and touched the cold iron rail of the bunk.

Rabia gave up her bed, I thought to myself. She packed her trunk and she went back to the tire shop.

I looked at Aqsa, who was marking a paragraph with a yellow highlighter.

"Aqsa?"

"What?"

"I am not packing my trunk."

Aqsa stopped her highlighter. She looked up over the rim of her reading glasses. For a second, just a fraction of a second, the hard, cynical armor around her eyes softened.

"Good," she said, her voice dropping to a low, steady pitch. "Because if you pack your trunk, they win without even throwing a punch. But if you stay, you need to understand one thing: the six of us in this room are not roommates. We are a garrison. We share the salt, we share the tea, and when one of us is under fire, we all hold the line. Do you understand?"

"I understand," I said.

And for the first time since my father had left me at the wicket gate, I did not nod.

I looked her straight in the eyes, kept my neck straight, and let the word stand on its own.

The afternoon heat in Room 42 grew heavier by four o'clock, the sun hitting the west-facing brick wall and turning the room into a sauna.

Aqsa had gone to the department to attend a postcolonial literature seminar, leaving me alone on the wooden chair near the scarred study desk.

I sat there, looking around the twelve-by-fourteen room that was to be my home for the next two years.

On the wall above the upper bunk where my thin cotton mattress lay, someone had stuck a small, faded color photograph of Benazir Bhutto using dried wheat paste. The photograph had been torn from an old magazine; the edges were curled and yellow, but the late prime minister's dark eyes looked out from under her white silk scarf with an unyielding, regal defiance that seemed entirely out of place in a dilapidated hostel room over an open sewer.

Beneath the photograph, written in blue ballpoint ink directly onto the whitewashed brick, was a couplet from Habib Jalib:

*Deep jiss ka mehalat hi mein jaley,* 
*Woh sahar hum ko manzoor nahin.* 
*(The dawn that illuminates only the palaces of the rich,* 
*That dawn is not acceptable to us.)*

I touched the dry, raised ink of the couplet with the tip of my index finger.

How many young women had sat on this exact wooden chair, looking at those lines, before their spirits were broken by the sifarish system?

Around five in the afternoon, the door swung open.

Three girls walked into Room 42, carrying heavy cloth bags and plastic water thermoses.

First was **Saba**. She was wearing an ironed navy blue kameez, her hair pulled back into a severe, practical bun with two black hairpins. She was carrying a leather document folder stuffed with bank challans, administrative forms, and photocopied salary certificates.

She dropped her folder onto the wooden desk with a heavy sigh that sounded like an engine losing compression.

"They rejected the fee concession again," Saba said, not looking at me, speaking to the room at large with that flat, exhausted fury common to working-class students. "The Assistant Registrar said my father's land revenue record was missing a counter-signature from the Tehsildar in Vehari. I spent six hours standing outside his office. He was drinking green tea and discussing real estate prices with a property dealer from Bosan Road. When I asked him to stamp the form, he looked at his watch and told me office hours were over."

"Did you tell him tomorrow is the final deadline for the fee voucher?" asked the second girl who entered.

This was **Momina**.

Momina looked like an explosion of creative chaos. She was tall, with curly black hair that spilled out from a multi-colored hand-printed dupatta, charcoal smudges on her knuckles, and a canvas portfolio tucked under her arm that was larger than her torso.

"Of course she told him," Momina said, kicking off her leather sandals and collapsing onto the lower bunk. "And do you know what the Registrar told her? He said: 'Beta, if your father cannot pay forty-six thousand rupees, why did he send you to a university? Higher education is for people who can afford it. Go back to Vehari and open a sewing center.'"

Momina threw her canvas bag onto the floor, her dark eyes flashing with rage.

"One day, I am going to draw that man with the head of a hyena and paste three hundred copies across the front doors of the administrative block. Let the whole city see what a university registrar looks like when his skin is peeled off."

Then, the third girl stepped into the room.

This was **Sidra**.

She walked slowly, pressing a cold, wet handkerchief against her right cheek. Her face was pale, her eyes clouded with intense physical pain.

"Did the dispensary have the antibiotic, Sidra?" Saba asked, looking up from her fee papers.

Sidra sat on the edge of her cot, shaking her head slowly.

"The dispensary dispenser said they have had no dental antibiotics since August," Sidra whispered, her voice muffled by the handkerchief. "He had only Panadol tablets. He gave me two loose tablets wrapped in a piece of newspaper. He told me to go to Nishtar Hospital OPD if the tooth was infected. But the OPD closes at one o'clock, and the rickshaw fare to Nishtar is three hundred rupees."

She closed her eyes, a single tear slipping past the edge of the handkerchief.

"My uncle called my mother this morning," Sidra whispered into the hot room. "He told her that if I spend all my father's pension on dental bills and books, there will be nothing left for my dowry. He said a girl with bad teeth and too many books is impossible to marry off."

I sat on the wooden chair, watching these three women.

In the official promotional brochures published by Bahauddin Zakariya University: glossy pamphlets printed on imported art paper with full-color photographs of smiling female students walking across green lawns under blooming jacaranda trees, none of this existed.

There were no rejected fee concessions for honest farmers' daughters. There were no tooth infections left untreated because the dispensary budget had been stolen. There were no arrogant registrars telling brilliant girls to open sewing centers.

The university brochures showed the facade: clean, progressive, meritocratic.

Room 42 showed the reality: an engine of attrition designed to grind down the spirit of working-class women until they surrendered their ambition and went quietly back to their villages.

Saba finally noticed me sitting near the desk. She adjusted her glasses, looking at my green trunk and my new notebooks.

"You must be Anam," she said, her voice softening slightly. "Aqsa told us a new girl was allotted Rabia's bed."

"Yes," I said, standing up. "Anam Sajjad. Political Science."

Saba looked at me for a long moment, her eyes assessing my clothes, my posture, and the way I held my chin.

"You have that look in your eyes, Anam," Saba said quietly. "The merit look. You think if you study sixteen hours a day, if you memorize the syllabus, if you write clean exam papers in neat blue ink, the university will reward you."

She walked over to the desk, picked up her rejected fee concession form with its red rejection stamp, and held it out to me.

"Look at this paper, Anam. I had ninety-four percent in intermediate. Top marks in the entire district. And this registrar wouldn't give me five minutes of his time because my father drives a tractor on another man's land. In this place, merit is the bait on the hook. Once they get your admission fee, the only thing that protects you is power."

"Then we will build our own power," I said.

The words came out of my mouth before I had time to calculate the risk.

The room went completely quiet.

Momina sat up on her mattress, her wild hair falling over her forehead, studying my face with sudden, intense interest. Sidra lowered her handkerchief, her swollen jaw catching the yellow light from the window. Saba stood holding her rejected paper, her eyebrows raised in disbelief.

"Build our own power?" Momina laughed softly, but there was no mockery in her voice; there was a spark of something dangerous and alive. "With what, Anam? With our empty pockets? With our broken ceiling fans?"

"With our data," I replied, pointing to Saba's folder. "With your drawings, Momina. With Aqsa's pen. With our refusal to apologize for being in this room. If we fight them individually, they pick us off one by one. They send Rabia back to the tire shop. They force Sidra into an unwanted marriage. They make Saba drop out over a fee voucher. But if we stand together in this corridor, if we refuse to nod to their threats, they cannot evict all six of us without causing a strike that brings the provincial education minister to this campus."

Momina looked at Saba. Saba looked at Sidra.

For a long moment, the only sound was the droning scrape of the ceiling fan and the crickets outside the window.

Then, Momina reached down, picked up a piece of black charcoal from her canvas bag, and walked over to the whitewashed wall next to Benazir Bhutto's faded photograph.

With quick, bold strokes, she drew an image directly onto the brick:

A small, fierce bird with human eyes, snapping an iron snare with its beak.

Beneath the bird, Momina wrote in neat black block letters:

**ROOM 34: NO SURRENDER.**

She turned back to us, blowing the charcoal dust from her fingertips.

"Welcome to the garrison, Anam Sajjad," Momina said, her face breaking into a wide, brilliant, defiant grin. "Now let us boil some water and plan how we are going to make that registrar regret the day he touched Saba's form."

### The Strategist's Margin: Tactical Protocols for Daring Women

#### Protocol 5: The Asymmetry of Discretion (How to Maneuver Without Sifarish)

1. **Never Rely on Unwritten Administrative Promises:** When an administrator tells you, "Do not worry, beta, submit the paper and I will handle your fee concession later," they are neutralizing your immediate resistance with an unwritten promise they have no intention of keeping. In Pakistani institutions, verbal sympathy is the cheapest sedative available. Demand everything in writing: diary numbers, receipt stamps, and photocopied acknowledgments. If they refuse a stamp, follow up with a registered postal letter. A postal receipt cannot be deleted from a ledger.
2. **The Defense of the Collective Unit:** When you lack individual political leverage, your only defense is horizontal solidarity. Corrupt administrations thrive by isolating dissenters, convincing each girl that she is alone in her struggle. Form an unbreakable information pact with your immediate circle. Share every administrative encounter, every subtle threat, and every fee irregularity immediately. When five women speak the exact same truth at the same time, the cost of silencing them multiplies exponentially.
3. **Refuse the Victim Mindset:** Recognizing that the system is rigged is not an excuse to despair; it is the prerequisite for strategic victory. Do not spend your emotional energy mourning the death of meritocracy. Accept the battlefield as it exists, master the regulations better than the clerks who enforce them, and operate with cold, disciplined precision.
4. **The Audit of Exploitative Conditions:** Document the material conditions of your allotment. If basic services (ventilation, clean water, structural safety) are denied based on socioeconomic discrimination, build an objective photographic and written record. Institutional exploiters fear external audits more than moral arguments.

5. **The Strategic Counter-Audit of Administrative Rejections:** When a bureaucratic gatekeeper rejects an application for an institutional entitlement (fee concessions, scholarships, housing allotments, thesis approvals), never accept an unwritten verbal dismissal. Demand a formal, written rejection memorandum specifying the exact statutory clause under which the denial was executed. Bureaucrats routinely issue oral rejections to conceal arbitrary discretion; when forced to create a written, appealable administrative paper trail, their courage collapses.
6. **The Psychology of Collective Defiance:** Notice how predatory systems maintain dominance through the atomization of grievance. # Chapter 5: The Circle of Seven (Room 42 Secrets)

By nine in the evening, the heavy heat inside Room 42 had settled into a dense, humid swamp.

Outside, the crickets in the eucalyptus trees screamed in a deafening, continuous pitch that sounded like high-voltage electric wires short-circuiting. The yellow bulb in the center of the ceiling flickered every time the compressor of the water cooler in the corridor kicked on, dimming to an orange wire before flaring back to life.

Room 42 was designed for four, stretched to six, and on most nights, functioned as the secret sanctuary for seven.

We called ourselves the Circle of Seven.

We came from seven different corners of Pakistan, from districts where girls were rarely allowed to see the inside of a high school, let alone an ancient university on Bosan Road. Each of us carried a public persona: the quiet student, the diligent notetaker, the obedient daughter who lowered her gaze in the seminar hall.

And each of us carried a secret that could have destroyed our lives if it reached the warden's desk.

First was **Aqsa**.

To the faculty of the English Department, Aqsa was a sharp, unsmiling student who wrote essays on postcolonial resistance and sat in the front row. To the hostel administration, she was a troublemaker who questioned water bills.

Her secret was hidden inside the hollow plastic tube of a broken desk lamp in the corner of Room 42. Inside that tube was a micro-SD card containing six issues of *The Red Gate*: an anonymous, photocopied two-page newsletter that appeared under hostel doors every three months. *The Red Gate* documented the exact figures of the hostel mess budget, the names of contractors who paid kickbacks to the assistant registrar, and the unreported cases of harassment by laboratory attendants. Aqsa wrote every word using an old offline tablet, printed the pages on an inkjet printer in Chungi No. 9 at midnight, and slid them under the doors while the wardens were asleep.

If she was caught, it would not be a hostel fine. She would be expelled under the university's anti-sedition bylaws and blacklisted from civil service examinations forever.

Second was **Anza**.

Anza was from a small village near Bahawalpur, studying sociology. She was the quietest girl in the room, with large, sorrowful eyes and a voice so gentle you had to lean close to hear her speak. She never complained about the watery daal in the mess. She never asked for tea.

Her secret was tucked inside a tin biscuit box beneath her iron cot. In 2023, her father's small cloth printing shop in the bazaar had gone bankrupt when the textile mill cancelled their credit. Her family was living on dry bread and borrowed flour. To pay her semester fee and send five thousand rupees home every month for her younger sister's school books, Anza stayed awake from midnight until four in the morning, sewing intricate shadow-work embroidery onto designer lawn shirts for the wives of senior professors.

She earned two hundred rupees per shirt. Her fingers were criss-crossed with tiny needle punctures that had turned blue under the skin. If the hostel warden discovered she was running an unauthorized tailoring trade inside Amna Hall, she would be evicted within an hour for running a commercial enterprise on campus.

Third was **Saba**.

Saba was a third-year economics major from Vehari. She was an academic machine. While other girls chatted in the corridor, Saba sat on her cot with three highlighters in her hand, memorizing statistical formulas until her lips moved without sound.

Her secret was a stolen spiral notebook locked inside her suitcase. It was a handwritten grading ledger from the Department of Economics that she had found left behind on a lecturer's desk during exam evaluation. The ledger proved, with mathematical certainty, that female students were systematically given lower internal assessment scores than male students, regardless of their test performance, to ensure that the gold medals and university teaching assistantships went to male graduates who were expected to become breadwinners. Saba was quietly building a statistical correlation model using five years of department records, waiting for the exact moment to drop the file onto the Higher Education Commission's portal.

Fourth was **Momina**.

Momina was from Sahiwal, studying fine arts. She had wild, curly hair that escaped from her dupatta, ink stains on her thumbs, and a laugh that made the hostel guards outside the window grumble about female modesty.

Momina's secret was stitched directly into the underside of her cotton mattress. If you turned the mattress over and sliced a three-inch section of the yellow seam, you found thirty-five brilliant, satirical charcoal caricatures of the university administration: the Vice Chancellor sitting on a throne of broken water coolers, the Chief Warden counting bribes with three hands, and Dr. Qureshi lecturing to a lecture hall populated entirely by bowing sheep. They were masterpieces of political satire, drawn on heavy cartridge paper. In an authoritarian institution, political satire is considered treason.

Fifth was **Sidra**.

Sidra was from a conservative landed family near Khanewal, studying Urdu literature. She was gentle, deeply religious, and observant of every social propriety.

Her secret was the white envelope with the red seal that she kept beneath her pillow. Her paternal uncle, who controlled her deceased father's agricultural land, had finalized her engagement to a man eighteen years older than her, a cousin with two previous marriages and a reputation for drunken violence in the village. The wedding was scheduled for the end of the semester. Sidra had secretly made contact with an independent legal aid shelter in Lahore through a junior female lawyer in Multan. She was preparing to file a petition of legal autonomy the day her final exam paper was submitted, knowing that it meant she could never return to her village, never see her mother again, and would have to enter a state of total social exile to save her life.

Sixth was **Iqra**.

Iqra was a master of logistics from Lodhran, studying public administration. She was short, sharp-witted, and possessed a network of contacts that rivaled the university intelligence bureau.

Her secret was the hollow wooden base of the common study desk. Inside that cavity, wrapped in plastic medicine pouches, was an underground pharmacy and supply depot: ventolin inhalers, antibiotics, sanitary pads, emergency painkillers, and duplicate gate passes with forged hostel stamps. In Amna Hall, where the dispensary closed at three in the afternoon and sick girls were left to shiver with typhoid on concrete floors, Iqra's underground pharmacy was the only reason three dozen girls had survived the winter without hospitalization. She charged nothing; she traded medicine for library books, fruit, and silence.

And seventh was **Palwasha**.

Palwasha was not formally allotted to Room 42. She lived down the hall in Room 41, but spent almost every evening sitting on our floor with her back pressed against my green trunk. She was from the tribal belt near Dera Ismail Khan, studying international relations on a provincial quota scholarship. She was tall, with high cheekbones, green eyes that looked straight through you, and a blunt directness that terrified the soft, polite girls from the Punjab plains.

Her secret was the burden of an ancestral blood feud that had claimed her father and two elder brothers when she was ten years old. Her family had sent her to Multan not just for an education, but to keep the last educated child alive. Palwasha carried an iron nail file sharpened to a razor point inside the lining of her boot, and she slept with her door unbolted because she believed that locked doors only trap you when the hunters arrive.

These were the seven women who sat in Room 42 on that humid night in late August.

We were not a random collection of roommates. We were an underground resistance cell operating inside the belly of a patriarchal machine.

Around ten at night, Iqra stood up from the floor, clicked the bolt of the room door into place, and hung a dark dupatta over the frosted glass transom so no light would leak into the corridor.

From under her cot, Anza pulled out a small plastic container filled with dry tea leaves, half a cup of sugar, and two green cardamoms. From under my bed, I pulled the contraband immersion rod and the dented steel mug.

Aqsa plugged the extension cord into the single wall socket behind the wardrobe, her eyes fixed on the door handle.

The immersion rod hummed. Within four minutes, the water was boiling, filling the cramped, airless room with the rich, clean aroma of cardamoms and black tea.

Palwasha took seven small plastic medicine cups from Iqra's supply box and poured three sips of hot tea into each one.

We sat in a tight circle on the floor, our knees touching, our shoulders pressed together in the narrow aisle between the iron cots.

Sidra held her hot plastic cup between both palms, staring down at the dark amber liquid.

"My uncle called my brother today," she whispered. Her voice was trembling. "He told him that he has already purchased the bridal tent. He said that if I do not arrive in Khanewal by the fifteenth of June, he will send four men in a pickup truck to the main gate of BZU to take me home."

The room went completely silent. The only sound was the droning scrape of the ceiling fan and the crickets outside the barred window.

Aqsa looked up from her tea. Her jaw was tight, her eyes cold as flint.

"Let him send the pickup truck," Aqsa said, her voice steady and sharp as a scalpel. "The fifteenth of June is four days after the final political theory exam. You are not going to Khanewal, Sidra. You are taking the eight-thirty morning Metro bus from BZU to Chungi No. 9. From there, Iqra has arranged a ride to the Daewoo terminal. You will be on the motorway to Lahore before your uncle's pickup truck even crosses the Chenab bridge."

"And what happens when he files a police abduction report against my brother?" Sidra asked, tears slipping down her cheeks.

"He cannot file an abduction report for a twenty-three-year-old woman with a national identity card and a registered affidavit of independent consent," Palwasha said, her green eyes fixed on Sidra. "My cousin in Lahore works with the women's legal collective. The affidavit is already drafted. The day you arrive, the High Court issues a protection order. In my homeland, we fight with rifles. In this city, you fight with affidavits and bus tickets. But you do not surrender. You never surrender to a man who treats your life like a sack of wheat."

Sidra swallowed. She looked at Palwasha, then at Aqsa, then at me.

She wiped her tears with the corner of her sleeve. Slowly, she took a sip of the hot tea.

"You really think I can do it?" she whispered.

"You have already done it," I told her, resting my hand on her knee. "The moment you refused to pack your trunk in your mind, you crossed the river. Now we just have to move your body."

In that tiny, suffocating room, surrounded by razor wire, open sewage, and the constant threat of administrative ruin, we drank our three sips of tea.

We had no political patrons. We had no money. We had no sifarish from senior bureaucrats.

All we had was the iron circle of our loyalty, the secrets we guarded for each other, and the unshakeable certainty that none of us would ever nod alone again.

It was forged in the fire of shared danger.

By our fourth month together in Amna Hall, we had developed an operational synchronization that functioned with the precision of a guerrilla intelligence unit.

Every member of the Circle of Seven had a specific, designated operational role:

**Aqsa** was our political and ideological strategist. She analyzed university bylaws, monitored provincial political developments, and authored *The Red Gate*. She knew every loop in the Higher Education Commission's statutory rules, and she could dismantle a department chairperson's administrative memo in fifteen minutes using his own institutional precedents.

**Anza** was our financial auditor and anchor. Despite her family's crushing bankruptcy, she managed our collective emergency cash fund: five thousand rupees pooled from our meager allowances, hidden inside a hollowed-out Urdu dictionary on the second shelf of our room. She recorded every rupee spent on emergency medicines, photostat copies, and food rations in a tiny leather notebook using double-entry bookkeeping.

**Saba** was our quantitative and data specialist. She possessed an extraordinary mathematical mind. While professors lectured on abstract political theories, Saba was tracking the university's fiscal allocations: analyzing the disparity between student fee collections and faculty travel allowances, building statistical spreadsheets that proved where the syndicate was siphoning public funds.

**Momina** was our visual and psychological warfare officer. Her satirical drawings and underground posters were legendary across campus. She understood that authoritarian power is sustained through manufactured dignity; when you strip a corrupt bureaucrat of his dignity through devastating, accurate satire, his ability to inspire fear is permanently neutralized.

**Sidra** was our emotional sanctuary and moral compass. Having survived the terror of her uncle's forced marriage plot, she possessed a profound, quiet courage that stabilized the room during moments of extreme panic. She was our link to the legal aid networks in Lahore, and she managed our secure correspondence with external civil society organizations.

**Iqra** was our logistical and intelligence genius. She had built an informal alliance with the university's working-class staff: the sweepers, the canteen boys, the night watchmen, and the photostat operators. Because she treated them with genuine human dignity, they provided her with critical early warnings: upcoming hostel room raids, impending fee hikes, and internal administrative memos before they were ever officially posted on the department notice boards.

And **Palwasha** was our tactical enforcer and physical shield. Tall, fearless, and armed with the uncompromising code of the frontier, she ensured that no male student political wing, no corrupt campus guard, and no predatory gatekeeper could ever intimidate our perimeter without facing immediate, devastating physical and legal consequences.

It was ten-thirty at night. The hostel had been locked for five hours.

Suddenly, heavy, aggressive footsteps sounded on the concrete stairs of the East Wing, accompanied by the jangling of master keys and the sharp, angry voice of Mrs. Farhat, the Assistant Warden.

"Search Room 42 first!" Mrs. Farhat's voice boomed down the corridor. "The security supervisor received a tip that illegal literature and unauthorized electrical appliances are being operated in that room! Open the door immediately!"

On the wooden desk, Aqsa had forty freshly printed copies of *The Red Gate*, hot from the duplicator, documenting an unreported sexual harassment complaint against a senior professor of sociology.

On the floor, our contraband immersion rod was hummed softly inside a steel mug.

In the bottom of Momina's canvas bag lay six original, unreleased satirical caricatures of the Vice Chancellor.

Under normal circumstances, six students caught with that evidence would have been expelled, handed over to the police under anti-state agitation charges, and had their academic records permanently incinerated.

We did not panic.

We had rehearsed the extraction protocol three times.

In four seconds flat:

Palwasha stepped to the door, slid the top brass bolt into place, and planted her heavy boot against the base of the wood, creating an immovable human doorstop that no master key could bypass.

Iqra reached down, yanked the immersion rod's plug from the wall, plunged the hot copper coil directly into a bucket of cold saline water to hiss into steam, wrapped the cooled element in an oily rag, and dropped it into the hollow leg of the common study table.

Momina grabbed her satirical caricatures, slid them beneath the mattress of the lower bunk, and sat on top of the bed, holding an open copy of the Holy Quran in her lap.

Aqsa gathered the forty copies of *The Red Gate*, walked to the open casement window, and dropped them into a canvas laundry bag that hung from a thin nylon cord outside the third-floor ledge, suspended ten feet below in the dark shadows between the brick pillars where no inspection torch could reach.

And I sat at the wooden desk, opened my textbook on Comparative Constitutional Law, picked up a blue pen, and looked at the door.

Six seconds.

The master key rattled violently in the brass lock of Room 42. The lock clicked open, but the heavy door did not move an inch: Palwasha's boot was wedged against the frame like a steel wedge.

"Open this door immediately!" Mrs. Farhat shrieked from the corridor, banging her fists against the wood. "Open in the name of the administration!"

Palwasha took her time. She counted three silent beats in her chest.

Then, with smooth, deliberate slowness, she slid her boot back, released the top bolt, and pulled the door open six inches.

Mrs. Farhat stood in the hallway, flanked by two female security guards in blue uniforms carrying heavy iron torches and long wooden batons. Behind them stood Maasi Sakina, looking uncomfortable, holding a canvas ledger.

Palwasha looked down at her with cold, emerald-green eyes, her arms crossed over her chest.

"The meaning of the delay, Assistant Warden sahib," Palwasha said, her voice slow, deep, and dripping with icy contempt, "is that six female citizens of Pakistan were engaged in their evening religious prayers. Are you now issuing disciplinary citations for reciting Surah Rahman in a hostel room after dark?"

Mrs. Farhat stopped dead in her tracks.

She looked at Momina, sitting peacefully on her cot with her head covered, rocking gently over the open pages of the Holy Quran. She looked at Saba, taking notes in an econometrics ledger. She looked at me, reading constitutional law under a sixty-watt bulb.

The room smelled of rose water and clean linen.

There were no papers on the desk. There were no hot wires in the bucket. There was no illegal literature.

"We received a formal report that unauthorized political pamphlets were being stored in this room," Mrs. Farhat insisted, her face reddening as she stepped toward the wooden desk, pulling open the drawers.

The drawers contained only plastic rulers, highlighters, clean notebooks, and an unopened box of Panadol tablets.

"Who filed the report, Assistant Warden?" Aqsa asked, stepping forward, her hands clasped calmly behind her back.

Mrs. Farhat froze. Her hands hovered over the empty drawer.

"That is confidential administrative intelligence," she snapped.

"It is not confidential, Mrs. Farhat," Aqsa said, her voice dropping to a lethal, whisper-quiet frequency that made the two security guards look at the floor. "It was filed by your brother-in-law, the mess contractor Haji Manzoor, because he discovered that we know the laboratory batch numbers for the non-graded industrial fat he bought in Industrial Estate. If you want to search our beds, search them. But understand this: tomorrow morning at nine o'clock, Palwasha's cousin will file a formal writ in the High Court for unauthorized nocturnal harassment of female students during religious devotions, naming you personally as the first respondent."

Mrs. Farhat stared at Aqsa.

She looked at the two security guards, who had already taken two steps back toward the hallway, entirely unwilling to be named in a High Court harassment petition. She looked at Palwasha, who had not moved an inch from the doorway, her arms crossed, blocking the exit.

The Assistant Warden swallowed hard. Her gold bangles clinked nervously against her watch strap.

"Keep this room clean," she muttered, turning around and walking briskly toward the corridor. "And keep the volume down. Other students are trying to rest."

"Good night, Assistant Warden," Palwasha said, her voice echoing down the hallway.

She closed the heavy green door.

She turned the brass bolt.

For three seconds, the room remained dead silent.

Then, Momina closed her Quran, placed it reverently on the wooden shelf, and burst into silent, breathless laughter, burying her face in her pillow so her shouts would not carry through the transom.

Aqsa leaned against the wall, a triumphant, brilliant smile breaking across her face.

I reached out to the casement window, pulled the nylon cord, and hauled the canvas laundry bag containing the forty copies of *The Red Gate* back over the sill, placing them safely onto the wooden desk.

We did not say a single word.

We stood in our circle, our knuckles touching, our chests heaving with adrenaline.

We had faced their surveillance, we had faced their master keys, we had faced their threats of expulsion.

And we had held the line without moving an inch.

# Chapter 6: Khara Paani (The Saline Water)


Yet the memory that outshone all the hardships of Amna Hall was our sacred evening ritual: **The Walk to the BZU Main Gate**.

Every evening around seven o'clock, as the fierce Multan sun finally dipped behind the western mango orchards and the asphalt began to cool, the stifling rooms of Amna Hall became uninhabitable. The air inside smelled of dust and warm brick, and our minds were numb from academic theory.

That was when Palwasha would appear in our doorway, clapping her hands with childish glee: "Chalo larkiyo! Utho! The sun has set, the breeze is coming from the orchards, and the samosa vendor at the main gate is frying fresh batches!"

Within five minutes, the four of us, Anam, Anza, Aqsa, and Saba, accompanied by Palwasha, would emerge from Amna Hall.

The walk from Amna Hall to the BZU Main Gate on Bosan Road was nearly a mile and a half along the wide, tree-lined university avenue. The path was shaded by ancient eucalyptus and neem trees, whose leaves rustled gently in the southern twilight. On both sides lay the sprawling green lawns of the university, where the evening shadows stretched long and cool.

Those walks were our only sanctuary of pure, unadulterated freedom.

On that road, we were not daughters under surveillance or students under administrative threat. We walked five abreast, our arms linked together, laughing until our stomachs ached. Anza would mimic the pompous accents of visiting Islamabad professors, making us double over with laughter. Aqsa, relaxed beneath her black abaya, would tell hilarious stories about village jinj weddings in Muzaffargarh. Saba would share practical gossip about department scholarships, and Palwasha would skip ahead, picking fallen jasmine flowers to tuck behind our ears.

At the Main Gate, where the bustling traffic of Bosan Road roared past, we would gather around the street carts. We bought roasted corn rubbed with lemon and black salt, spicy potato samosas dripping with mint chutney, and chilled bottles of lemon soda that hissed when opened. We stood on the footpath, eating from brown paper bags, watching the colorful city buses speed toward Multan Cantt, feeling the immense, untamed pulse of the world outside.

We walked slowly back under the starlit sky, timing our arrival to eight-forty-five, just fifteen minutes before the heavy iron gates of Amna Hall were locked with chains for the nine o'clock curfew.

Those evening walks were our life support. They reminded us that despite the poverty of our institutions, despite our aching bodies, and despite the endless compromises demanded of us by society, our youth was alive, vibrant, and fiercely beautiful.



If you have never lived in southern Punjab, you do not know the taste of water that hates you.

In Multan, water is not a neutral liquid that falls from clouds or flows clean from mountain glaciers. In Multan, water is pulled from deep beneath the dry clay plains by diesel turbine borewells that reach four hundred feet into ancient subterranean salt beds.

The water that comes out of the iron pipes of Amna Hall is known across the campus simply as *khara paani*: the bitter saline water.

It is yellow-tinted, heavy with dissolved magnesium sulfate, sodium chloride, calcium carbonate, and the faint, unmistakable stench of rotten eggs that signals sulfur gas. When you turn on the rusted brass tap in the communal washrooms on the third floor, the water spits and coughs with a violent hiss of trapped air, splattering a warm, viscous spray against the cracked ceramic sink.

If you drink it unboiled, your intestines twist into knots within two hours, producing an agonizing, burning dysentery that leaves you dehydrated on a concrete floor. If you boil it on an immersion rod, a thick, chalky white crust of mineral salts precipitates onto the bottom of your steel mug, coating your teeth with a gritty film that tastes like lye and crushed limestone.

And if you wash your hair with it, your body begins to disintegrate.

Every morning at six, before the sun turned the concrete roof into an anvil, the communal washrooms of the East Wing resembled a field hospital after a siege.

There were twelve stalls for two hundred girls. Six of the stalls had broken wooden doors whose latches had been replaced with loops of twisted coat-hanger wire. Inside each stall stood a twenty-liter red plastic bucket and a cracked plastic mug.

The floor was a continuous slab of wet, unpolished grey concrete, slick with green algae near the drain and covered in fallen hair.

Not a few strands of hair. Bunches of hair. Thick, wet, dark tangles of female hair that clogged the iron drainage grates every forty-five minutes.

Every girl who stayed in Amna Hall for more than two semesters lost half her hair to the khara paani.

I remember sitting on my haunches in stall number four in my third month, pouring a mug of warm saline water over my head. The water burned my scalp, stinging the small sores that had erupted along my hairline from the mineral salts. As I worked a sliver of cheap Lifebuoy soap through my hair, I felt the strands coming loose in my fingers, sliding away from the roots without resistance, like wet silk unraveling.

When I looked down at my hands, my palms were covered in a thick, wet nest of my own hair.

I was twenty-one years old, and my thick, waist-length black hair, which my mother had oiled with mustard seed and brushed with wooden combs every Sunday since I was a little girl, was washing down a cracked concrete drain in Amna Hall.

I sat there on the cold concrete, clutching the wet hair in my trembling fist, and I began to weep.

From the stall next to me, I heard the sound of Palwasha's plastic mug clattering against her bucket.

"Anam?" her voice echoed off the damp ceramic tiles, sharp and resonant. "Are you crying in there?"

"My hair is falling out, Palwasha," I choked out, my voice cracking. "It is coming out in handfuls. Half my scalp is visible near the parting."

I heard the wire latch of her stall creak open. A moment later, Palwasha pushed open my door, completely unbothered by modesty. She stood there wrapped in a grey cotton towel, her green eyes fierce and clear through the sulfur steam.

She reached down, took my chin in her strong, calloused fingers, and tilted my face upward until I was forced to look directly at her.

"Listen to me, daughter of Sajjad," Palwasha said, her voice hard as river granite. "Look at my head."

She ran her wet hand through her own short-cropped hair. It had been shorn off at the jawline, rough and uneven, cut with a pair of paper shears.

"When I arrived here from the frontier, my braids reached my hips," she said. "The saline water made my scalp bleed. The warden told me it was my own fault for not buying imported shampoo from the mall on Bosan Road. Do you know what I did? I borrowed Iqra's scissors and I cut it off over the trash bin. My brothers cried when they saw the photograph. But I told them: I came to this university to get an education, not to be an ornament on a marriage stage. Hair grows back. A broken will does not."

She reached down, picked up the plastic mug, filled it from the bucket, and poured it firmly over my shoulders, washing away the fallen strands.

"Stand up," she commanded gently. "Wipe your eyes. We have a political theory seminar at eight-thirty. If you skip the lecture because of fallen hair, the warden wins and the sulfur wins. You do not let a cracked pipe defeat you."

I stood up. My knees were shaking, but I wiped my face with the hem of my cotton dupatta.

That morning in the washroom, as I watched Palwasha towel her short hair dry, I realized that the khara paani was not just a plumbing failure.

It was an ideological instrument.

The university administration had plenty of funds. Across Bosan Road, in the administrative block where the vice chancellor, the deans, and the registrars had their offices, an industrial reverse-osmosis filtration plant hummed twenty-four hours a day. The senior officers had chilled, bottled water delivered to their desks in five-gallon blue dispensers every Monday morning. Their personal washrooms had ceramic tiles, chrome faucets, and sweet canal water piped in from the private campus filtration system.

The saline bore water was reserved exclusively for the students, and in particular, for the female hostels.

Why?

Because a student body that is constantly fighting skin rashes, chronic digestive infections, kidney stones, and hair loss is a student body that has very little energy left to question university fees, administrative corruption, or missing library budgets.

When your daily life is consumed by the physical struggle to secure four liters of drinkable water from a broken cooler two floors down, you do not have the luxury to organize an academic boycott. When your body is poisoned by the very water you wash with, your physical exhaustion becomes the university's political peace.

Every evening at five, before the curfew bar fell across the green iron gate, a small, desperate procession formed outside the hostel wicket door.

Girls carrying empty one-point-five-liter plastic Pepsi bottles, oil jerrycans, and dented thermoses hurried across the pedestrian footbridge over Bosan Road to the public filtration plant installed outside the Metro bus station.

The queue stretched forty yards into the dust.

We stood there in the blistering heat, waiting our turn at the single stainless steel tap to fill our plastic bottles with clean water that would not burn our throats. If the Metro station guards were in a bad mood, they would turn off the main valve at five-fifteen, laughing as two dozen university girls begged through the fence for ten more minutes to fill their bottles before the hostel curfew locked them inside for the night.

I watched girls from wealthy families in Lahore and Islamabad who lived in the private hostels on Bosan Road drive past in air-conditioned cars, carrying cartons of mineral water on their back seats.

And I watched my roommates in Room 42: Anza boiling her water twice on the illegal immersion rod; Sidra pressing a hot saltwater cloth against her aching jaw; Iqra distributing rehydration salts from her contraband pharmacy; and Saba drinking straight from the saline tap with her eyes shut, swallowing the sulfur because she did not have twenty minutes to waste standing in the Metro station queue when she had two chapters of econometrics to read before midnight.

We were drinking the poison of institutional neglect every single day.

And yet, every time the Chief Warden visited Amna Hall during her monthly inspection, flanked by her two assistant wardens in spotless chiffon saris, she would ask:

"Are the daughters of the hostel comfortable? Is the water pressure adequate?"

And sixty girls gathered in the common room, with their scalps burning and their throats dry from sulfur, would lower their eyes, fold their hands in front of their dupattas, and nod.

*Yes, madam. Thank you, madam. Everything is fine.*

It was that nod that made the water bitter.

The water was saline because the earth of Multan is ancient and full of salt. But the water remained in our pipes, rotting our hair and poisoning our kidneys for decades, because not one generation of women had possessed the organized courage to throw a bucket of that yellow, sulfurous sludge onto the polished marble floor of the Vice Chancellor's office.

The human body is eighty percent water, which means that within sixty days of moving into Amna Hall, your physical tissues become an archive of the geological faults of Multan.

The soil of this ancient city has absorbed the salt of dried prehistoric inland seas, the ash of five thousand years of burned brick kilns, and the agricultural chemical runoff of forty years of intensive pesticide spraying in the surrounding cotton belt.

When the university borewell pumps pull that water to the surface, they do not just pull water; they pull a chemical cocktail of heavy metals, dissolved gypsum, and nitrates.

By our fourth month, every girl in Room 42 had developed her own medical vocabulary for the damage.

Anza had developed chronic, weeping eczema on her knuckles from the laundry soap reacting with the magnesium salts, her skin splitting into raw red fissures that made her wince every time she picked up her needle.

Sidra suffered from recurrent kidney gravel: small, jagged calcium oxalate crystals that tore through her urinary tract every three weeks, leaving her curled in a fetal position on her iron cot, biting her pillow to keep from screaming while Iqra boiled cups of fennel seed water to soothe the spasms.

And I watched my hairline recede half an inch from my forehead, my scalp covered in burning follicular cysts that throbbed with every heartbeat.

One Friday morning in December, I walked across the campus to the Department of Chemistry to meet a junior lecturer named Dr. Nabila.

Dr. Nabila was a quiet, brilliant woman of thirty-four who had completed her doctorate in water chemistry at a German university before returning to Pakistan on a government repatriation scholarship. She worked in a small, damp laboratory in the basement of the science faculty, surrounded by glassware, titrators, and humming spectrophotometers.

I placed a two-liter plastic Pepsi bottle on her laboratory bench. The water inside had a distinct yellow-green tint and smelled faintly of a struck match.

"What is this, Anam?" Dr. Nabila asked, adjusting her safety spectacles.

"Tap water from the third floor of Amna Hall, East Wing," I replied. "Two hundred girls wash with it and cook their rice in it every day. I want to know what is in it."

Dr. Nabila looked at the bottle, then looked toward the laboratory door to ensure the corridor was clear. She pulled the bottle toward her, unscrewed the blue plastic cap, and took a cautious, practiced sniff.

Her face tightened.

"Leave it with me until Monday," she said quietly. "Do not tell anyone you brought this to my lab."

On Monday afternoon, I returned to the chemistry basement.

Dr. Nabila was sitting at her wooden desk, looking at a printed spectral analysis sheet. The laboratory was dark except for the green glow of an analytical balance.

She slid the printed report across the bench to me.

"Total dissolved solids: two thousand four hundred parts per million," she said, her voice completely devoid of emotion, the voice of a scientist reading an autopsy. "The World Health Organization maximum guideline for human consumption is five hundred parts per million. Your tap water is five times above the emergency safety threshold."

I looked at the numbers:

*Sulfates: 680 mg/L (WHO limit: 250 mg/L)* 
*Chlorides: 740 mg/L (WHO limit: 250 mg/L)* 
*Electrical Conductivity: 3,800 µS/cm* 
*Total Hardness: 920 mg/L as CaCO3 (Extremely Hard)* 
*Fecal Coliform Count: 48 CFU/100 mL (Safety Standard: 0 CFU/100 mL)*

My stomach twisted into a cold knot.

"Fecal coliform?" I whispered.

"Sewage," Dr. Nabila said bluntly, resting her pencil on the paper. "The main northern campus drainage canal runs twenty feet from the foundation of the East Wing. The underground brick sewer pipes were installed in 1978. They have cracked under soil subsidence. The sewage from the nullah is seeping through the clay and contaminating the shallow suction casing of your hostel borewell pump. You are not just drinking salt water, Anam. You are washing your faces with diluted campus sewage."

I stared at the laboratory sheet. The numbers blurred before my eyes.

"Why doesn't the administration replace the suction casing?" I asked, my voice trembling with fury. "The university collects millions in hostel maintenance fees every single semester!"

Dr. Nabila smiled: a sad, bitter, hopeless smile that broke my heart.

"Because replacing that borewell casing costs eight hundred thousand rupees, Anam," she said, leaning across the desk. "And eight hundred thousand rupees spent on underground pipes cannot be inaugurated with a ribbon. It cannot be photographed for the newspaper. A new fountain in front of the Vice Chancellor's office, or new air-conditioners for the syndicate lounge, those can be photographed. Invisible infrastructure for female students is an administrative deadweight loss in their budget."

She reached out, took the laboratory sheet from my hands, folded it in four, and slid it back into my handbag.

"Do not drink this water, beti," she said, her eyes full of maternal concern. "If you drink this for four semesters, your renal filtration will be permanently compromised. Carry your water from the Metro station plant, no matter how heavy the bottles are. Your kidneys will not get a second chance."

I walked out of the chemistry building into the blinding winter sunlight.

I held my handbag against my ribs, feeling the sharp, stiff edges of that folded laboratory report pressing against my skin.

That piece of paper was not just a chemical water analysis.

It was an indictment of the entire moral architecture of the Pakistani state.

The state demands that its daughters be modest, that they be obedient, that they study hard, that they preserve the family honor, and that they accept their domestic roles without protest.

And in return for that total, lifelong obedience, the state pipes diluted sewage into their washrooms and tells them to be grateful for the gift of higher education.

I walked toward the elevated pedestrian bridge of the BZU Metro station.

I looked at the long queue of girls standing at the public water filtration tap, holding their plastic bottles in the dust, waiting their turn in the cold wind.

I did not join the queue.

I took out my spiral notebook. I sat on the concrete steps of the pedestrian bridge, resting the paper on my knees.

I transcribed every single chemical number from Dr. Nabila's report: the sulfates, the chlorides, the electrical conductivity, the fecal coliform count.

Beneath the numbers, I wrote:

*They poison our bodies and call it discipline.* 
*They destroy our organs and call it culture.* 
*We will not drink their sewage, and we will not nod to their clean water fountains.*

My neck was straight. My hand did not shake.

The water in our pipes was bitter with salt and rot, but the ink on my page was pure, unadulterated fire.

# Chapter 7: The Mess Token and the Midnight Ration

If the water in Amna Hall was an assault on our biology, the mess was a masterclass in economic extortion.

The mess hall was located in the basement of the West Wing, directly underneath the communal washrooms. It was a vast, low-ceilinged hall of raw concrete pillars, dimly lit by four humming fluorescent tube lights whose plastic covers were filled with dead moths and black grease. The air in the basement was thick with the suffocating smell of stale hydrogenated vegetable oil, burnt wheat chaff, and the sweet, sickly rot of decomposing vegetable peelings from the grease trap outside the kitchen door.

To receive food in Amna Hall, you did not use cash.

Cash allows for market competition. In Amna Hall, the administration operated a closed command economy designed to extract the maximum revenue from the poorest students while guaranteeing a fifty percent profit margin for the private catering contractor.

The contractor was a man named Haji Manzoor.

Haji Manzoor was fifty-eight years old, with a dyed red beard, a white prayer cap perched on his bald crown, and a protruding belly that strained the mother-of-pearl buttons of his embroidered white kurta. He was the brother-in-law of a former member of the university syndicate, which meant that his ten-year catering contract had been renewed four times without a public tender, despite eighty-two formal complaints filed by resident students over food poisoning outbreaks.

On the first day of every month, every resident of Amna Hall had to stand in a two-hour queue outside Haji Manzoor's glass booth in the basement to purchase the Mess Coupon Booklet.

The booklet cost seven thousand five hundred rupees. It contained sixty perforated cardboard coupons: thirty pink coupons for lunch, thirty green coupons for dinner.

The rules printed on the back of the booklet in red Urdu ink were simple, brutal, and non-negotiable:

*1. Coupons are valid only for the designated month and cannot be carried forward.*
*2. Unused coupons will not be refunded under any circumstances.*
*3. If a student is absent due to illness, exams, or family emergency, the coupon is deemed consumed.*
*4. Food cannot be removed from the mess hall in private containers.*
*5. Any student found using contraband cooking appliances in hostel rooms will face immediate room cancellation and a five-thousand-rupee fine.*

Haji Manzoor had constructed a perfect monopoly. You were forced to pay for thirty days of food in advance, regardless of whether you ate it, and you were legally prohibited from cooking your own food in your room.

And what did the seven thousand five hundred rupees buy?

At twelve-thirty in the afternoon, lunch was served.

You stood in a queue holding a dented aluminum tray divided into three shallow compartments. At the serving counter stood two kitchen boys in grease-stained shirts and rubber boots, sweating profusely over giant aluminum cauldrons that looked like tar kettles.

Into your largest compartment, the boy would slap a ladle of boiled white rice: broken grains, yellowed with age, sticking together in a lukewarm, starchy clump that smelled of warehouse burlap and weevils.

Into the second compartment went the daal.

It was called *daal chana*, but it had no relation to chickpeas. It was ninety-five percent yellow water, colored with cheap industrial turmeric powder, with five or six hard, unboiled split peas resting at the bottom like gravel. On top of the liquid floated a half-inch slick of rancid *Dalda* vegetable oil that turned white and waxy the moment the air conditioning breeze touched it.

If you chewed carefully, your teeth would inevitably strike a small stone or a piece of uncleaned grit that made your jaw ache.

Into the third compartment went the roti.

The roti was rolled from the cheapest brown flour bought from subsidized government utility stores, filled with coarse bran and chaff. It had been baked four hours earlier in a wood-fired tandoor, stacked eighty high in wicker baskets, and left to steam in its own moisture until the dough turned cold, grey, and rubbery as vulcanized truck tire.

To tear that roti, you had to grip it with both hands, plant your thumbs into the crust, and pull with the strength of a weightlifter. If you let it sit on your tray for ten minutes, it hardened into a brittle frisbee that could crack a concrete tile if dropped.

On Tuesday nights, Haji Manzoor served the legendary *Murgh Salan* (chicken curry).

The entire hostel knew the truth about the chicken. It was bought from the wholesale poultry markets outside Multan Cantt at six in the morning: dead-on-arrival broilers, bruised poultry, and discounted neck and back pieces that restaurants refused to purchase. Each girl received one bony neck piece or a single hollow wing floating in a bowl of fiery red chili oil that scorched your esophagus and burned your stomach lining until sunrise.

I remember sitting at the long wooden mess table on a Tuesday evening in November.

Anza was sitting opposite me. She had been coughing for three weeks, a dry, racking tubercular wheeze that shook her thin ribs. She picked up a piece of the grey roti, dipped it into the watery yellow daal, and brought it to her mouth.

She tried to chew. Her eyes watered. Slowly, she put the piece back onto the aluminum tray.

"I cannot swallow it, Anam," she whispered, her voice trembling with exhaustion. "It tastes like wet cardboard and machine grease. My throat is on fire."

"You have to eat something, Anza," I urged her, pushing my own untouched roti toward her. "You have had nothing since eight in the morning except two dry cream biscuits. You will faint during the public finance exam tomorrow."

She looked down at the aluminum tray. A single tear fell from her cheek, landing directly into the waxy yellow pool of daal.

"Do you know how my father earned the seven thousand five hundred rupees for this coupon booklet?" she said quietly, her knuckles tightening around her spoon. "He sold our water buffalo calf. The calf was three months old. He carried it to the mandi in a hired loader rickshaw, sold it to a butcher for nine thousand rupees, and sent the bank draft to my hostel account. He thought he was buying his daughter good, nutritious food in the city. He thinks I am eating chicken and eggs every day."

She buried her face in her cotton dupatta, her shoulders shaking with silent, breathless sobs.

Sitting two seats down from us was Iqra.

Iqra did not cry. Iqra possessed the cold, analytical intelligence of a guerrilla commander operating behind enemy lines.

She reached across the wooden table, picked up Anza's aluminum tray, picked up my tray, and stood up.

She walked straight to the kitchen disposal drum near the exit: a massive, rusted oil barrel filled with swill, where two stray cats were fighting over chicken bones. With two sharp, deliberate motions, Iqra dumped both trays of food directly into the swill barrel.

The kitchen supervisor, a short man with a pencil behind his ear named Bashir, shouted across the room:

"Oi! What are you doing? Food is a blessing of God! Why are you wasting it?"

Iqra turned around slowly. She did not raise her voice. She did not yell. She walked over to Bashir's counter, rested her knuckles on the aluminum railing, and looked him dead in the eyes.

"That is not food, Bashir," Iqra said, her voice cutting through the noise of the basement like a razor blade. "That is sewage. And if you ever speak to my roommates in that tone again, I will carry a sample of that rancid Dalda directly to the district food safety inspector at Kutchery Chowk, with the original laboratory invoice showing that Haji Manzoor is buying non-graded industrial fat from the soap factory in Industrial Estate. Would you like to see that invoice, Bashir?"

Bashir's face went white as curd. He looked at the floor, picked up a dirty rag, and began wiping an already clean counter without saying another word.

Iqra walked back to our table, grabbed Anza's hand, grabbed my arm, and pulled us toward the stairs.

"Come," she commanded. "We are going to Room 42."

Upstairs, behind our locked door, with the dark dupatta pinned over the transom and the illegal immersion rod humming in the blue bucket, the real economy of Amna Hall went to work.

From under her cot, Momina produced a half-loaf of fresh sandwich bread that she had bought from a bakery outside Chungi No. 9 and smuggled inside her drawing portfolio. From her locked trunk, Saba produced a tin of processed cheese spread and a plastic jar of red chili pickle. From my own green trunk, I produced three boiled eggs that I had purchased from an old street vendor on Bosan Road.

We boiled water in the dented steel mug. We made seven cups of piping hot black tea with cardamom. We toasted the bread on the flat metal lid of an empty biscuit tin placed directly over the glowing coils of the immersion rod.

We sat on the floor, eight inches from each other, passing around pieces of warm, buttered toast, slices of hard-boiled egg sprinkled with black pepper, and cups of sweet, steaming tea.

Anza ate. She chewed slowly, her eyes closed, her color returning to her cheeks as the hot tea warmed her throat.

"This is better than Haji Manzoor's entire banquet," she whispered, smiling for the first time in three weeks.

"Haji Manzoor's banquet exists for one reason," Aqsa said, taking a sip from her plastic cup. "To teach you that you have no rights. When you accept garbage on an aluminum tray and say thank you, you are practicing for the rest of your life. You are practicing for the corrupt government department that withholds your salary. You are practicing for the in-laws who demand your paycheck while serving you yesterday's leftovers. Every cold roti you swallow without protesting is a brick in the wall of your own prison."

She looked at each of us in turn, her sharp eyes gleaming in the dim yellow light.

"Never get used to the taste of bad food, girls. The day you stop noticing that the daal is full of stones is the day your soul surrenders."

We finished our tea in silence.

Down in the basement, Haji Manzoor was locking his cash register, counting his thick bundles of thousand-rupee notes, secure in the knowledge that sixty girls had eaten his rancid Dalda and said nothing.

He did not know that three floors above him, in Room 42, seven girls had just declared economic independence over a slice of toasted bread and a contraband cup of tea.

The economics of Haji Manzoor's mess hall was a textbook case of sovereign rent-seeking in a captive market.

Every semester, twelve hundred girls resided across the three female hostels of Bahauddin Zakariya University: Amna Hall, Fatima Hall, and Zainab Hall.

Each resident was compelled, by mandatory administrative regulation, to pay seven thousand five hundred rupees per month for the mess coupon booklet.

That represented a guaranteed, non-refundable monthly revenue of nine million rupees: nine million rupees deposited directly into Haji Manzoor's private bank accounts before a single grain of rice was purchased in the wholesale grain market at Ghalla Mandi.

And how did Haji Manzoor fulfill his contractual obligations?

Through the systematic, forensic degradation of nutritional value.

One Sunday morning in January, under the cover of the morning fog, Iqra and I conducted an economic audit of the mess kitchen's procurement invoices.

Iqra had spent two weeks cultivating a professional alliance with Chacha Ramzan: the fifty-year-old night watchman who guarded the delivery gate behind the kitchen. Chacha Ramzan was a diabetic whose monthly salary of sixteen thousand rupees was routinely delayed by the catering contractor. Iqra provided him with weekly blood sugar monitoring from our room and regular supplies of metformin from her underground pharmacy.

At six in the morning, while the hostel was asleep, Chacha Ramzan unlocked the kitchen delivery door and let us into the pantry.

The pantry was a dark, damp concrete cellar that smelled of rotting onions, stale flour, and diesel exhaust from the nearby delivery trucks.

We did not touch anything. We carried small penlight torches and our mobile phone cameras.

Sitting on wooden pallets near the wall were sixty burlap sacks of flour.

I walked over and inspected the printed stencil on the coarse burlap:

*PUNJAB FOOD DEPARTMENT | UTILITY STORE SUBSIDIZED FLOUR | NOT FOR RESALE.*

It was low-grade, subsidized government flour, heavily cut with coarse wheat bran, meant for rural famine relief programs, purchased by Haji Manzoor at a seventy percent discount from corrupt food inspectors in Khanewal, while billing the university students for premium, whole-wheat flour.

Next to the flour sacks stood twenty metal drums of cooking oil.

I wiped the dust from the label with my thumb:

*ROYAL REFINED INDUSTRIAL SHORTENING | FOR INDUSTRIAL SOAP & BAKERY USE ONLY | NON-GRADE EDIBLE OIL.*

It was crude palm oil sludge, containing high levels of free fatty acids and industrial trans-fats, purchased from the chemical refineries in the industrial estate on the bypass. When heated in the kitchen cauldrons, it released acrid, carcinogenic fumes that coated the basement walls with thick, sticky brown grease.

And in the walk-in cold storage room, which was barely cool because the refrigeration compressor was broken, hung fifty carcasses of spent layer hens: old, exhausted egg-laying chickens from commercial poultry farms outside Kabirwala, slaughtered when their egg production dropped to zero, their meat tough, stringy, and full of concentrated antibiotic residues.

Haji Manzoor's entire food operation was a criminal enterprise operating in plain sight.

The actual procurement cost of the daily food served to each resident: the watery daal, the rubbery brown roti, the rancid vegetable oil, was less than seventy-two rupees per day.

Yet each student was charged two hundred and fifty rupees per day.

Haji Manzoor was extracting a net monthly profit of over six million rupees from twelve hundred female students, while their fathers borrowed money from village moneylenders and sold their water buffalo calves to pay the vouchers.

I stood in that dark, freezing pantry, watching Iqra systematically photograph every fraudulent invoice, every industrial oil drum label, and every government utility flour stencil.

My hands were shaking, not with cold, but with an icy, absolute fury.

This was why Anza was coughing violently into her pillow, her respiratory allergy triggered by the acrid, burnt-grease fumes drifting into Room 42 from the mess chimneys. This was why Sidra's gums were bleeding from scurvy and nutritional deficiency. This was why sixty girls in Amna Hall had chronic gastritis, peptic ulcers, and iron-deficiency anemia that left them exhausted on their examination benches.

Our bodies were being mined for cash by a political syndicate that used our hunger to fund their private real estate investments on Bosan Road.

"Did you get the invoice for the poultry delivery, Iqra?" I whispered.

"Every single sheet," Iqra replied, her phone camera clicking in the dark. "Stamped by the veterinary transit officer at the Khanewal toll plaza: *Condemned Poultry Lot No. 412. Released for Poultry Meal Processing.* He fed us condemned broiler waste, Anam. He fed us food that is legally prohibited for human consumption."

# Chapter 8: The Metro Encounter (Meeting Yousaf)

A heavy elbow caught my left shoulder.

The cardboard folder slipped from my numb fingers.

# Chapter 10: The Photostat Archives (Chacha Asghar's Den)

Behind the Department of Political Science, tucked beneath the outdoor concrete staircase that led to the faculty air conditioning compressors, was a narrow brick alcove eight feet wide and twelve feet deep.

It smelled of burnt ozone, hot toner powder, cheap machine grease, and stale filterless Gold Flake cigarettes.

This was the intellectual engine of the university.

Above the wooden doorframe hung a rusted tin sign, painted by hand in chipped blue enamel: *AL-MADINA PHOTOSTAT & COMPOSING CENTRE. CHACHA ASGHAR.*

Inside the den, barely illuminated by a single bare sixty-watt bulb hanging from a fraying cloth wire, sat two massive, grey Canon photocopiers from the late 1990s. Their metal casings were dented and scratched, held together in places with silver duct tape. They ran continuously from eight in the morning until six in the evening, humming with a rhythmic, mechanical drone: *shhk-clack, shhk-clack, shhk-clack*, spitting out warm, static-charged sheets of white paper into plastic trays.

Behind the machines stood Chacha Asghar.

He was a man of sixty-five with a snow-white beard stained yellow around his mouth from four decades of smoking, thick cataract spectacles held together with copper wire at the nose bridge, and ink-blackened thumbs that looked like they had been dipped in liquid coal.

Chacha Asghar possessed more real power over the academic fate of five thousand students than the Dean of the Faculty of Arts.

He did not have a degree. He had left school in the fifth grade in a village near Shujabad. But he possessed the Photostat Archives: sixty wooden shelves crammed with cardboard cartons, labeled in blue marker with the names of every course taught at Bahauddin Zakariya University since 1986.

If you wanted to pass Comparative Political Systems without attending a single lecture, you did not go to the library.

You walked down the concrete steps behind the department, pulled aside the faded calico curtain of the photostat den, placed two hundred and fifty rupees on the toner-stained counter, and said:

"Chacha. Professor Qureshi. Five-year guess paper."

Chacha Asghar would not even look up. His blackened hand would reach into the third carton from the left on shelf number four, pull out a twenty-page stapled sheaf of papers, and slap it onto the counter.

Inside that sheaf was the entire examination paper you would face in December.

Not a sample paper. Not a practice test. The exact questions, phrased in the exact same broken English, accompanied by the exact five-point model answers that Dr. Qureshi had written in 2004 and had never updated since.

For thirty-five years, the faculty and the students had maintained an unspoken, corrupt truce mediated by Chacha Asghar's photocopiers:

The professors pretended to teach; the students pretended to learn; Chacha Asghar sold the answers for two hundred and fifty rupees; and the examination branch printed the degrees on thick parchment paper stamped with the university seal.

Everyone passed. Everyone nodded. The university's graduation statistics looked magnificent in the annual report.

And the graduates walked out onto Bosan Road with a Master's degree in Political Science, unable to draft a single professional memorandum, unable to calculate a percentage variance in a public budget, and entirely incapable of critical thought.

It was an industry of manufactured illiteracy.

I spent three hours inside Chacha Asghar's den on a Thursday morning in early May, waiting for him to photocopy eighty pages of historical land reform gazettes that I had borrowed from the provincial archives.

Zahra was from a lower-middle-class family in Muzaffargarh. Her father ran a small bicycle repair stall on the bypass. By her third semester, the department had broken her spirit.

She was sitting on the wooden bench, her head bowed, clutching a fresh copy of the *Five-Year Solved Guess Papers for International Relations*. Her eyes were red-rimmed, dull with that heavy, vacant exhaustion that comes when an idealist realizes she has been running inside a hamster wheel.

"Are you preparing for the mid-terms, Zahra?" I asked, sitting beside her on the bench.

She looked down at the stapled sheets in her lap. The paper was rough, cheap pulp, and the toner ink was already smudging onto her fingers.

"What is the point of preparing, Anam?" she whispered. Her voice was flat, devoid of anger, which made it far more terrifying than rage. "In the mid-term paper for Political Philosophy last week, I wrote six pages analyzing how Machiavelli's advice in The Prince reflects the modern bureaucratic mechanics of the civil secretariat in Lahore. Zahra had surrendered. Rabia had packed her trunk. A thousand girls before them had memorized Chacha Asghar's five-point model answers, bowed their heads, collected their degrees, and vanished into the silent obscurity of domestic subjugation.

### The Strategist's Margin: Tactical Protocols for Daring Women

# Chapter 11: The Strike, The Tear Gas, and The Suspended Metro

The riot began on a scorching Monday morning in late May, during the peak of the university mid-term examination week.

It started at ten o'clock outside the administrative block, where two rival student political organizations: the student wing of the ruling provincial political party and an armed ideological student federation, clashed over the lucrative monopoly on university transport contracts and hostel canteen concessions.

In a Pakistani public university, a student clash is not a civilized campus debate.

It is an armed factional turf war conducted by young men carrying automatic Kalashnikov rifles, steel knuckle-dusters, and iron reinforcement rods stripped from construction sites, operating under the direct patronage of provincial ministers and local criminal syndicates.

Within twenty minutes, the sound of semi-automatic gunfire was echoing across the main campus boulevard, cracking through the heavy summer heat like dry bamboo splitting in a fire.

Inside Amna Hall, two hundred and fifty female students were trapped like sheep inside a fortified sheepfold.

At ten-fifteen, the heavy green iron gate was slammed shut by six campus security guards wearing steel riot helmets. They dropped three massive horizontal iron bars across the double gates, wrapped a heavy ship's anchor chain through the iron handles, and secured it with two heavy brass padlocks.

Then the security guards took off their helmets, laid their batons on the grass, climbed into an open security jeep, and drove away toward the administrative block, leaving Amna Hall locked from the outside with no staff, no warden, and no medical personnel inside the compound.

The only person left at the gate was Maasi Sakina, who sat on her wooden charpai with her hands over her head, reciting the verses of Surah Yaseen in a cracked, terrified voice.

At ten-thirty, the wind shifted.

A contingent of Punjab Police anti-riot squads, armed with wooden shields and tear gas launchers, had cornered eighty rioting students on Bosan Road directly outside the university perimeter wall.

The police fired thirty rounds of tear gas canisters into the crowd.

The heavy, acrid canisters, trailing plumes of dense white chemical smoke, arced over the twelve-foot brick boundary wall of Amna Hall. Two of them landed directly on the hard-packed clay of our inner courtyard, while a third struck the concrete balcony of the second floor, hissing and spitting chemical fire onto the hanging laundry.

Within ninety seconds, Amna Hall was turned into an outdoor gas chamber.

The chemical agent used by the anti-riot police was not standard crowd-control gas; it was expired CS tear gas from military surplus stocks. It smelled of burnt industrial pepper, rotten cabbage, and chlorine gas.

The moment the white cloud touched your face, your mucous membranes felt as though they had been bathed in boiling battery acid.

Your eyes clamped shut with violent, involuntary spasms. Blind, searing tears flooded your cheeks. Your nasal passages constricted until you could not draw air through your nose, and when you opened your mouth to gasp for oxygen, the chemical smoke entered your throat, triggering uncontrollable, gagging dry heaves that dropped girls to their knees on the concrete corridors.

Two hundred girls began screaming in the dark stairwells.

Doors were banging. Footsteps thundered across the concrete floors as terrified students ran from the suffocating lower corridors toward the upper floors, crying for water, calling for their mothers, convinced that the hostel was being stormed by armed militants.

In Room 42, **Palwasha** took command within three seconds.

While Sidra was choking on her cot and Anza was hyperventilating into her pillow, Palwasha leaped across the room, grabbed our five-liter plastic water container, and dumped it directly over six heavy cotton dupattas that were hanging from the clothesline.

"Do not rub your eyes!" Palwasha roared, her voice cutting through the panic like an artillery shell. "If you rub your eyes, you grind the chemical crystals into your corneas! Tear the wet cloth! Cover your nose and mouth! Press it tight against your face!"

She shoved a soaking wet, cold cotton dupatta into my hands.

I pressed the wet cloth over my nose and mouth. The cold water filtered the worst of the burning gas, allowing me to draw a shallow, painful breath into my burning lungs.

Palwasha grabbed a second wet cloth and tied it firmly around Sidra's head, covering her mouth. She pulled Anza from her cot, forced her down onto the concrete floor where the air was marginally cooler, and shoved a third wet cloth into her face.

Aqsa was already at the casement window.

She had pushed the heavy wooden shutters closed and was stuffing wet socks and old newspaper sheets into the cracks around the window frame to keep the white chemical fog from entering the room.

"They have locked the main gate from the outside!" Aqsa shouted through her wet mask. "I saw the guards drive away! We are padlocked in!"

I crawled on my hands and knees over to the window and peered through a narrow crack in the shutter.

Down in the courtyard, the white tear gas smoke was billowing across the hard-packed earth like thick winter fog. The three stunted neem trees were completely obscured. Near the wicket door, a discarded tear gas canister was still sputtering sparks, charring a hole in a fallen cotton dupatta.

And outside on Bosan Road, visible through the razor wire on the perimeter wall, the city had stopped.

The elevated track of the Multan Metro Bus system was completely deserted. The automated stations at BZU terminal and Chungi No. 9 had their emergency steel shutters pulled down. The red-and-green buses were parked in long, motionless rows along the concrete viaduct, their diesel engines shut off, their glass windows reflecting the black plumes of smoke rising from two burning motorcycle rickshaws on the highway below.

The university administration had declared an indefinite emergency curfew.

The entire city of Multan was locked down.

For four agonizing days and four nights, Amna Hall remained under total, unbroken siege.

No food arrived. The mess hall in the basement remained padlocked, Haji Manzoor having fled to his bungalow in Cantt the moment the first gunshot was fired.

On the second morning, the municipal power supply to the northern campus was severed when a high-voltage transformer outside the department was destroyed by an arson attack.

The electricity died.

When the electricity died, the electric bore pumps died with it.

By noon on the second day, the taps in the communal washrooms let out a final, choking gasp of air and went bone-dry. Not a single drop of saline water flowed through the iron pipes.

Two hundred and fifty girls were trapped inside a concrete fortress in forty-six-degree summer heat, with no electricity, no running water, no sanitation, and no access to the outside world.

Cell phone signals were jammed by the district administration to prevent student factions from coordinating reinforcement mobs. Every time a girl held her phone to the barred window, the screen read: *No Service.*

It was in those ninety-six hours of total abandonment that the veneer of modern civilization peeled away, revealing the raw, primal reality of institutional neglect.

Girls were drinking the stale, rust-colored water from the bottoms of the communal toilet cisterns. Third-floor residents were fainting from heat exhaustion and dehydration, lying motionless on the concrete corridors with wet cloths over their foreheads.

In Room 42, the Circle of Seven did not faint.

We went onto a military war footing.

Under **Iqra's** logistical direction, we conducted an emergency inventory of our survival assets:

We had eighteen liters of clean drinking water stored in plastic bottles beneath our iron cots.

We had half a sack of dry wheat rusks that Anza had purchased two days before the strike.

We had two jars of peanut butter, three packets of powdered milk, half a kilo of brown sugar, and a dozen packets of oral rehydration salts from Iqra's contraband pharmacy.

We formed a defense perimeter around Room 42.

Palwasha stationed herself near the door with her sharpened steel nail file and a heavy iron reinforcement rod that she had secretly pried loose from a broken balcony railing on the third floor.

Every six hours, under cover of darkness, Aqsa, Momina, and I would leave Room 42 carrying two plastic mugs and a small medical kit.

We moved quietly along the suffocating, pitch-black corridors of the East Wing, checking on the youngest girls: first-semester students from small rural towns who were weeping in their rooms, paralyzed by terror, their lips cracked and bleeding from dehydration.

We administered measured doses of rehydration salts dissolved in three ounces of clean water. We washed the eyes of girls whose corneas had been burned by the tear gas crystals using sterile saline solution from Iqra's stash. We distributed dry rusks, breaking them into quarters so that forty girls could have a single mouthful of solid food before sunrise.

We did not ask for money. We did not ask for merit marks. We did not ask which political party their brothers supported.

In the dark, suffocating heat of that besieged hostel, with the smell of rotting sewage rising from the dried washroom pipes and the silence of the suspended Metro overhead, we kept forty young women alive through pure, disciplined horizontal solidarity.

On the fifth morning, at six o'clock, the roar of diesel engines broke the campus silence.

Three army troop carriers and two armored police vehicles escorted a convoy of private transport buses through the shattered main gates of BZU.

The heavy anchor chain on Amna Hall's green iron gate was cut with an industrial bolt-cutter. The iron doors swung open with a screech of rusted hinges.

The Chief Warden walked into the courtyard, flanked by four armed police officers and two junior lecturers carrying clipboards. She wore a pristine white lawn suit, oversized sunglasses to protect her eyes from the morning glare, and held a perfumed handkerchief over her nose to block the stench of the open drains.

"Attention, residents!" her voice boomed through a battery-powered megaphone. "Pack your bags immediately! The university is closed indefinitely by order of the Syndicate! You have thirty minutes to vacate the compound and board the evacuation buses to the general bus stand! Any student remaining after seven o'clock will be arrested under Section 144!"

Two hundred girls poured out of the dark stairwells like ghosts, hollow-eyed, trembling, dragging their dented trunks across the gravel path, weeping with relief as they climbed into the evacuation buses.

The Chief Warden stood by the wicket door, checking names off her clipboard, nodding with bureaucratic satisfaction at the efficiency of the evacuation.

When the six of us from Room 42 walked down the concrete stairs carrying our belongings, we did not run.

We walked slowly, shoulder to shoulder, our spines straight, our chins held level with the horizon.

Palwasha carried her leather duffel bag. Iqra carried her medical kit. Anza carried her embroidery frame. Sidra carried her artist portfolio containing her court injunction. Saba carried her econometrics ledger. Momina carried her rolled-up satirical caricatures. And I carried my green tin trunk and my unlined spiral notebook.

We reached the wicket gate.

The Chief Warden looked up from her clipboard. She saw our faces: clean, unbowed, without tears, without the frantic, begging hysteria of the other residents.

"You girls from Room 42," the Warden said, her voice sharp with institutional annoyance. "Why were you not at the gate when the horn sounded? Don't you know there is an emergency?"

Aqsa stopped. She did not lower her chin. She looked at the Warden through the tinted glass of her sunglasses.

"We were busy doing your job, madam," Aqsa said, her voice quiet, steady, and terrifyingly clear. "We were keeping your students alive while you were hiding in your air-conditioned bungalow in Cantt. You do not need to hurry us. We are leaving. But do not ever mistake our departure for your victory."

The Chief Warden's mouth opened in shock. Her face turned crimson under her powder.

"What is your roll number, girl?" she hissed. "I will have you expelled before the sun sets!"

"Write it down, madam," Palwasha said, stepping forward, towering over the Warden with her green eyes blazing like emeralds. "Her roll number is 402. Mine is 418. And if you touch a single hair on her head, fifty files documenting how your mess contractor bought industrial soap fat will be on the desk of the National Accountability Bureau in Lahore before your tea gets cold. Take your clipboard and step aside."

The Warden looked at Palwasha's face. She looked at the seven of us standing in an unbreakable, immovable wall of young womanhood.

She saw no fear. She saw no weakness. She saw no willingness to nod.

Slowly, without saying another word, the Chief Warden of Amna Hall took one step backward onto the gravel.

We walked through the green iron gate.

We crossed the threshold without bending our spines. We walked out onto Bosan Road, where the morning sun was rising behind the concrete pillars of the Metro station, casting long, golden shadows across the highway.

The red line above us was still silent, but as we walked together toward the dawn, we knew that the real power in Multan was no longer on the elevated tracks or in the air-conditioned syndicate rooms.

The real power was walking on foot, carrying an unlined spiral notebook, ready to write the truth into the history of this country.

Aqsa, Palwasha, and I followed them down the dark, suffocating corridor.

Fatima Hall was a scene from an urban apocalypse.

We realized that during an institutional breakdown, the people who claim official authority: the deans, the wardens, the syndicates, vanish into their air-conditioned sanctuaries.

The only authority that remains real, the only authority that possesses moral legitimacy, is the authority of the women who stay in the dark, share the water, and refuse to let each other perish.

# Chapter 12: The 48-Degree Summer & Rooftop Secrets

In southern Punjab, summer is not a season. It is an occupation.

By mid-June, the temperature in Multan climbs past forty-eight degrees Celsius in the shade. The wind that blows off the dry clay wastelands of the Thal and Cholistan deserts is known as the *loo*: a blistering, invisible furnace blast that sucks the moisture from your eyeballs within fifteen seconds.

If you touch a metal doorknob at two in the afternoon, it burns the skin of your fingertips like a hot griddle. The birds in the eucalyptus trees do not fly; they sit motionless in the deepest shadows with their beaks hanging open, panting for air.

Inside Room 42 on the third floor of Amna Hall, the concrete roof had been absorbing the direct radiation of the sun for fourteen hours a day since May.

The concrete ceiling radiated heat downward like the ceramic bricks of a baker's oven. Even at eleven at night, when the sun had been down for three hours, the air inside our room hovered at forty-two degrees. If you placed your hand against the concrete wall, it felt warm to the touch, as though a fire was burning behind the plaster.

At midnight on the twentieth of June, the main campus transformer outside the department blew up with a deafening, metallic roar that shook our window frames.

A fountain of blue sparks lit up the sky, followed by the pungent stench of burning PCB dielectric oil.

The electricity died instantly.

The two ceiling fans in Room 42 spun down, their droning scrape dying into a terrible, suffocating silence.

Within ten minutes, Room 42 became completely uninhabitable.

The air was so hot and thick that breathing felt like inhaling steam from a boiling kettle. Sweat poured from our foreheads, dripping into our eyes, soaking our cotton shirts until they were plastered to our ribs. Anza was sitting on the floor, gasping for breath, her face crimson, her pulse hammering visibly in her throat.

"We cannot stay in this room," Saba whispered, wiping sweat from her eyelids with the back of her forearm. "The carbon dioxide is trapped. We will suffocate before morning."

Aqsa walked to the door and pulled it open.

The corridor was a nightmare of screaming heat. Dozens of girls were wandering the dark hallway in their cotton vests, holding hand fans woven from date palm leaves, their faces pale with heat exhaustion.

At the end of the East Wing corridor was a heavy iron door with a rusted padlock, barring the flight of concrete steps that led up to the flat roof of the hostel. The administration kept the rooftop padlocked under strict disciplinary rules, claiming it was for female moral protection and to prevent suicides during exam weeks.

Standing in front of the padlocked door was **Palwasha**.

In her right hand, she held the two-foot iron reinforcement rod that she had salvaged during the May riots.

She looked at the thirty girls gathered in the dark stairwell.

"Stand back," Palwasha commanded.

She wedged the chisel-tipped end of the iron rod into the hasp of the brass padlock. She planted her right foot firmly against the brick wall, gripped the iron lever with both hands, and threw the entire weight of her body into the fulcrum.

A sharp, violent crack of snapping brass echoed through the stairwell.

The rusted padlock sheared in two, falling onto the concrete steps with a hollow clatter.

Palwasha kicked the heavy iron door open with the heel of her boot.

A sudden, breathtaking rush of open night air swept over us. It was still hot: thirty-six degrees, but it was moving air, carrying the scent of night-blooming jasmine from the distant canal and the dry dust of the open plains.

Two hundred girls poured out of the dark, suffocating stairwells onto the vast, flat brick expanse of the Amna Hall rooftop.

The roof was as large as a football pitch, bordered by a three-foot brick parapet. High above us, free from the yellow glare of city streetlamps, the southern sky was an immense, clear dome of deep indigo velvet, dusted with millions of blazing white stars.

We dragged our light cotton quilts, our ralli rugs, and our woven plastic mats up the concrete stairs.

Within an hour, the rooftop was transformed into an open-air city of women.

Two hundred female students lay under the open sky, their heads resting on canvas bags, their dupattas spread across the cooling bricks, listening to the vast, quiet breathing of the sleeping earth.

In the far corner of the roof, behind the water tank tower where the parapet wall blocked the wind, the Circle of Seven gathered on a faded red carpet that Momina had brought from her room.

In the center of our circle sat a dented tin tray.

On the tray were six massive, golden-yellow Chaunsa mangoes from Anza's family orchard near Bahawalpur: the king of Multan's fruit, smelling of honey, sunshine, and rich river loam.

Momina pulled a small, dull folding knife from her pocket. With practiced, elegant strokes, she sliced the mangoes into thick, dripping golden crescents, laying them neatly along the edge of the tin tray.

We ate with our fingers, the sweet, sticky nectar running down our wrists, licking the sweetness from our knuckles, laughing softly in the dark so our voices would not carry down to the courtyard where Maasi Sakina lay sleeping on her charpai.

"Eat, Anza," Palwasha said, handing her the largest slice. "Your family's water buffalo calf may be gone, but your soil still knows how to produce gold."

Anza smiled, her teeth gleaming white in the starlight. She took a bite, closing her eyes as the taste of her home village filled her mouth.

"Do you know why my father's printing shop went bankrupt?" Anza whispered into the night. Her voice was quiet, steady, free of the shame that had weighed her down for three semesters.

We leaned close, our shoulders touching in the dark.

"A textile exporter in Multan ordered twenty thousand meters of block-printed cotton," Anza said, looking up at the Milky Way. "My father borrowed three hundred thousand rupees to buy the unbleached fabric and the natural dyes. He worked sixteen hours a day for three months. When the shipment was delivered to the warehouse on the bypass, the exporter's manager smiled, accepted the delivery, and handed my father a post-dated check for sixty days."

She paused. A cool breeze rustled the hem of her dupatta.

"When my father went to the bank in Cantt on the sixtieth day, the check bounced. The exporter had liquidated the holding company and transferred the assets to his son's offshore firm. When my father went to the police station to file a complaint for fraud, the sub-inspector told him: 'Sethi sahib is a donor to the ruling party. If you file this report, I will lock you in the lockup for filing a malicious petition against a respectable businessman.' My father bowed his head, tore up the police application, and came home. That night, he sold the printing blocks for scrap iron."

Anza looked at her needle-punctured fingers, tracing the small blue marks under the starlight.

"That is why I sew shirts for the professors' wives until four in the morning. Not because I like embroidery. But because my father bowed his head to a thief, and I swore to myself that no Sethi in this city will ever make my younger sister drop out of school. I will sew until my fingers bleed, but my sister will finish her degree."

Aqsa reached out and closed her fingers over Anza's scarred palm.

"They will not defeat your sister, Anza," Aqsa said, her voice hard as steel. "Because we are putting Sethi's holding companies into *The Red Gate*. Next month's issue has the complete corporate registry of the university syndicate members, including the textile mills that pay zero property tax to the municipal corporation while our hostel taps run with sulfur. People are going to read it."

"Where do you print it, Aqsa?" Momina asked, leaning forward on her elbows. "If the police catch you at a commercial printing press, they will trace the invoice."

Aqsa pulled a small brass key from a string around her neck.

"I don't use commercial printing presses," she said, a wicked, triumphant grin spreading across her face. "I use the high-speed digital duplicator in the office of the Dean of Arts."

We stared at her in stunned, breathless disbelief.

"You use the Dean's machine?" Iqra gasped, her hand flying to her mouth to stifle a laugh.

"Every second Thursday at eight in the evening," Aqsa whispered, laughing silently. "The night sweeper, Chacha Boota, has a diabetic daughter who needs insulin every month. Iqra provides the insulin from her pharmacy. Chacha Boota leaves the Dean's side office unlocked for twenty-five minutes while he sweeps the main corridor. I walk in with my master stencil, run three hundred copies on the university's official A4 paper, pack them into my backpack, and walk out. The university pays for its own resistance. It is the only efficient administrative program they run."

We collapsed into muffled, uncontrollable giggles, pressing our faces into our dupattas so our laughter would not alert the night guards.

Momina wiped tears of laughter from her cheeks. She reached behind her back and pulled a rolled cardboard cylinder from under the carpet.

"Look at this," she said, unrolling a large sheet of heavy cartridge paper under the starlight.

It was a charcoal drawing of Dr. Qureshi.

He was depicted standing at his wooden podium in Seminar Room 4, but instead of human arms, he had the massive, mechanical scissors of a sheep shearer. Before him stood a long line of young women wearing university graduation gowns, each waiting her turn to have her tongue clipped off with the shears and dropped into a wooden collection box labeled: *FOR THE PEACE OF THE STATE.*

At the bottom of the drawing, Momina had written in sharp, calligraphic Urdu:

*The degree is not an honor. The degree is a receipt for your tongue.*

The drawing was devastating in its brilliance. It was political art of the highest order: fierce, unsparing, and entirely true.

"What will you do with it?" I asked, staring at the charcoal lines.

"I am going to exhibit it at the National College of Arts in Lahore for my graduate thesis," Momina said, her chin lifted with fierce pride. "The chairperson told me that if I display political art that criticizes the academic establishment, I will never receive a teaching appointment in a public university in Punjab."

"And what did you say?" Palwasha asked.

"I told him: 'Sir, I came to this university to become an artist, not a court painter for your syndicate.' If they will not give me a teaching job, I will paint murals on the brick walls of the old city until the municipal council whitewashes them. But I will never paint a smiling face on a rotting wall."

Palwasha raised her piece of Chaunsa mango in salute.

"A woman from Sahiwal with the blood of a warrior," Palwasha said. "In my mountains, we would write a ballad about you."

She looked at me, sitting quietly on the corner of the red carpet with my unlined spiral notebook in my lap.

"And you, Anam?" Palwasha asked. "What is your secret? We know you write until four in the morning. We hear your pen scratching the paper while the immersion rod hums. What are you building in that notebook?"

I looked down at the rough, off-white pages.

I looked at the stars blazing above us, at the two hundred sleeping girls spread across the vast brick roof, and at the green-and-white Metro bus tracks gleaming in the distance along Bosan Road.

"I am writing our names," I said quietly.

"Our names?" Sidra asked, touching my knee.

"In all the books taught in this university, our names do not exist," I said, feeling the words rise from the deepest part of my chest. "In the political science syllabus, there are generals, there are prime ministers, there are British viceroys, and there are American theorists who never spent a day in fifty-degree heat. There is not a single sentence about the girl who boils sulfur water on an illegal immersion rod to study for her exams. There is not a single sentence about the girl who escapes a forced marriage with a Metro bus ticket and a court injunction. There is not a single sentence about the price we pay when we nod to men who are destroying our country."

I pressed my palm flat against the notebook.

"I want the world to know that we were here. I want my name: Anam Sajjad, printed on the cover of a real book that cannot be confiscated by a warden or burned by a syndicate. I want every girl who comes after us to know that when she enters Seminar Room 4, she is not the first woman who refused to bow her head."

The six of them looked at me.

Nobody laughed. Nobody told me I was ambitious, or arrogant, or foolish.

Aqsa picked up the final slice of Chaunsa mango from the tin tray and handed it to me.

"Then finish the book, Anam," Aqsa said, her voice soft with an unshakeable faith that made my heart ache. "Write every word. Write about the khara paani. Write about the mess tokens. Write about the tear gas. And when the book is printed, we will walk into the Vice Chancellor's office together, seven women with our heads held high, and we will place it on his polished mahogany desk."

We sat on that rooftop until four in the morning, while the earth cooled and the desert wind softened into dawn.

Seven girls on a brick roof in Multan, bound by secrets that could have destroyed us individually, but made us completely invincible together.

# Chapter 13: The Breakdown (The Unknown Disease & Nishtar Wards)

The human nervous system is an exquisite, terrifying instrument of truth.

You can lie to your father. You can lie to your professors. You can arrange your facial muscles into a submissive smile and nod thirty times an hour to satisfy a department chairperson.

You cannot lie to your spinal cord.

Every time you swallow a scream of righteous rage, the chemical charge does not vanish into the air. It retreats into your tissues. It binds to your neurotransmitters, constricts your peripheral micro-capillaries, and deposits a microscopic layer of inflammatory stress into the sheath of your peripheral nerves.

For two years in Amna Hall, I had been an Olympic athlete of emotional suppression.

I had swallowed the disgust when Dr. Qureshi made suggestive comments about the female students' clothes during tutorial hours. I had swallowed the fury when the warden stole our medical emergency budget. I had swallowed the terror of living in fifty-degree heat with no running water.

By November of my second academic year, my body presented the final invoice.

The collapse began on a cold Wednesday morning during a departmental seminar on constitutional law in Seminar Room 4.

I was standing at the wooden podium, presenting a critique of the doctrine of state necessity in Pakistani jurisprudence. My notes were laid out neatly on the lectern. I was speaking with clarity, citing historical Supreme Court judgments from 1958 and 1977.

Suddenly, without warning, the vision in my right eye went completely black.

It was not a gradual fading. It was as though someone had dropped a heavy black velvet curtain over the right side of my face.

A wave of intense, nauseating vertigo hit my brain, tilting the floor fifty degrees to the left. At the exact same instant, a blinding, electric shock: like someone had plunged a two-hundred-and-twenty-volt live wire into the base of my skull, shot down my cervical spine, radiating into my shoulders and exploding into my fingertips.

My right hand, which was resting on the edge of the wooden lectern, began to jerk violently with an uncontrollable, rhythmic spasm.

The papers in my hand fluttered to the floor.

"Anam?" Dr. Qureshi's voice came from the front row, distant, echoing as though he was shouting from the bottom of a dry well. "What is the matter with you? Continue the presentation."

I opened my mouth to speak.

No sound came out. My tongue felt thick, heavy, and wooden, like a piece of dead meat wedged in my pharynx.

My knees folded beneath me like wet cardboard.

I hit the concrete floor behind the wooden podium with a sickening, heavy thud. My head struck the edge of the blackboard, and then the darkness closed in from both sides, dragging me down into a cold, silent ocean.

When I opened my eyes, I was lying on an iron trolley in the emergency casualty ward of Nishtar Hospital.

Nishtar Hospital is the largest tertiary medical facility in southern Punjab. Built in the 1950s to serve five hundred patients, it currently processes four thousand human beings a day.

The emergency ward was a circle of Dante's Inferno paved in cracked terrazzo tiles.

The air smelled of carbolic acid, old blood, human vomit, and the sickening, sweet stench of gangrene from the diabetic ward down the hall. On either side of my trolley, patients were groaning on rusted iron beds without sheets: an old farmer with a crushed leg from a threshing machine; a young girl from Shujabad with chemical burns from an acid attack; an infant gasping for air inside a plastic oxygen hood that was misted with yellow condensation.

Standing beside my trolley was my father.

He had traveled three hours on a local bus from our district the moment the department clerk called him. He was still wearing his work clothes, his coat covered in dust, clutching his black umbrella and his wooden prayer beads, his eyes wide and wild with terror.

"Anam," he wept, leaning over the iron guardrail, pressing his calloused, trembling hand against my forehead. "My daughter. What happened to you? The doctor said you had a fit in the university."

"It was not a fit, Baba," I whispered, the words coming slow and slurred through my numb lips. "My neck. My spine is on fire. I cannot feel my fingers."

For three weeks, Nishtar Hospital subjected my body to a battery of medical diagnostic torture:

They plunged two-inch steel needle electrodes into the muscles of my forearms and calves to test nerve conduction velocity, sending pulsing electrical shocks through my tissues while an intern watched a green oscilloscope screen.

They drew eighteen vials of dark venous blood from my bruised arms, testing for lupus, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, myasthenia gravis, and heavy metal poisoning from Multan's saline water.

They strapped my head into an iron cage and slid me into the clanking, deafening tunnel of a magnetic resonance imaging scanner for forty-five minutes.

Every single test returned the exact same conclusion:

*Negative.*

*Brain parenchyma: Normal.* 
*Spinal cord caliber: Within normal limits.* 
*Electromyography: No evidence of motor neuropathy.* 
*Sedimentation rate: Normal.*

Biologically, medically, on paper, I was in perfect health.

Physically, I was a cripple.

I could not lift a two-liter plastic water jug without blinding pain shooting down my arms. My right hand had developed a permanent, resting tremor that made handwriting impossible. Every afternoon at three o'clock, a low-grade, burning fever of one hundred and one degrees would ignite in my blood, accompanied by a crushing, vice-like migraine that made the smallest sliver of daylight feel like an ice pick driven through my temples.

On the final morning of my admission, the Head of the Department of Neurology, Professor Dr. Aslam, arrived for grand rounds.

He was flanked by eight young house officers in white coats, all carrying plastic clipboards. He wore a three-piece tweed suit imported from England, gold-framed spectacles, and carried himself with the supreme, bored arrogance of a man who considers himself a deity in a province of illiterates.

He flipped through my thick medical file for sixty seconds. He did not look at my face. He did not ask where the pain was.

He turned to my father, who was standing beside my bed with his shoulders hunched in his perpetual posture of humble apology.

"Sajjad sahib," Professor Aslam said, his voice smooth, clinical, and completely devoid of empathy. "Your daughter has no organic neurological pathology. Her brain is normal. Her spine is normal. Her nerves are conducting signals at standard physiological velocity."

"Then why can she not walk without falling, doctor sahib?" my father begged, his hands folded in front of his chest. "Why are her hands shaking? Why is she burning with fever every afternoon?"

The great professor smiled: a patronizing, benevolent curl of his manicured lips that made my stomach turn to ice.

"It is a classic case of conversion disorder," he declared loudly, so all eight house officers could write the diagnosis on their clipboards. "Female hysteria. Somatization. When girls from traditional, rural backgrounds are sent to co-educational universities, the academic competition and the social environment create unconscious anxiety that their fragile psychological constitutions cannot process. The mind converts this emotional stress into simulated physical symptoms: pseudo-seizures, functional tremors, subjective pain. It is an attention-seeking mechanism."

I lay on that rusted iron bed, listening to this man dismiss my two years of agony as a theatrical performance.

"What is the treatment, doctor sahib?" my father asked, tears running into his white beard.

"Take her home," the professor said, handing the file back to his junior doctor. "Cancel her university registration. Discontinue all academic work immediately. Put her on a low-dose tranquilizer, keep her away from books and stressful conversations, and find a respectable boy to marry her. Once she is settled into a marital home with domestic responsibilities, the functional symptoms will resolve themselves. Women of her class are not biologically suited for intense intellectual labor."

He turned on his polished leather heels and walked out of the ward, his eight junior doctors following behind him like ducklings.

My father stood by my bed, his head bowed, his spirit completely broken.

He reached down and began gathering my medicine bottles into a plastic shopping bag.

"We are going home, Anam," he said, his voice hollow as an empty tomb. "The doctor is right. You have pushed yourself too hard. We will freeze your semester. We will go back to the village."

I looked at my right hand, trembling on the stained white bedsheet.

In that moment, lying on an iron bed in the charity ward of Nishtar Hospital, with the smell of death all around me and the contempt of the medical establishment ringing in my ears, I understood the ultimate weapon of the patriarchy:

When a woman's body breaks under the weight of an unjust system, the system will never blame itself.

It will call her crazy. It will call her hysterical. It will tell her that her pain is an illusion, her ambition is a neurosis, and her only cure is to return to the kitchen and submit to another man's authority.

If I had nodded to Professor Aslam that morning, I would have signed my own death warrant.

I would have returned to my district, taken his tranquilizers, sat in a dark room until my mind turned to jelly, and allowed myself to be handed over to a cousin who would have demanded my obedience until the day I was buried in the village graveyard.

Beneath the blinding migraine, beneath the burning cervical pain, beneath the trembling in my wrists, a cold, diamond-hard fury ignited in my soul.

*I am not hysterical,* I told my trembling fingers. *I am not crazy. I am poisoned by silence.*

My spine screamed with pain. The room tilted. Dark spots swam before my eyes.

I did not fall.

I looked at my father, who was tying the handles of the plastic shopping bag.

"Baba," I said. My voice was raspy, dry as sandpaper, but it carried a tone he had never heard from me in twenty-one years.

He stopped. He looked at my face.

"We will freeze the session for one year," I told him, looking straight into his tired eyes. "I will come home. I will rest my body. But understand this: I am not abandoning my degree. And I am not marrying anyone. In one year, I will walk back through the gates of BZU on my own two feet. And I will finish what I started."

My father stared at me. For five long seconds, the ward was completely quiet.

He saw the iron in my jaw. He saw that the docile, agreeable daughter who had nodded to every clerk in Multan was dead, killed by the khara paani and the sulfur and the medical contempt of Nishtar Hospital.

In her place stood an unbroken woman.

Slowly, gently, my father picked up my coat, draped it over my burning shoulders, and nodded once.

"Achha, Anam," he whispered. "We will fight it together."

# Chapter 17: Walking Bosan Road in 2026 (The Unbowed Head)

It is eight o'clock on a bright, crisp morning in February 2026.

I am standing on the elevated pedestrian overpass outside the main gate of Bahauddin Zakariya University, leaning my forearms against the stainless steel railing, looking down at the awakening pulse of Multan.

Down below on Bosan Road, the red-and-white motorcycle rickshaws rattle past in clouds of blue exhaust, their drivers weaving through the traffic with the reckless, joyous bravado of survivors. The vendor with the wooden cart is still there near the canal bridge, slicing sweet Kinnow oranges from Khanewal into bright red plastic baskets, shouting prices into the morning breeze.

And rising directly above the dust, majestic and gleaming in the morning sunlight, the concrete viaduct of the Multan Metro Bus system stretches toward the horizon like an unbroken silver spine.

A green-and-red articulated Metro bus hums quietly into the BZU terminal, its automated glass doors sliding open with a pneumatic sigh, releasing a wave of young women in dark abayas and bright dupattas onto the concrete platform.

I look at them: twenty-year-old girls clutching their plastic document folders, their eyes wide with that familiar mixture of terror and determination that I carried when I first dragged my green tin trunk through the gate four years ago.

I am twenty-four years old now.

Because of the one-year medical drop that nearly destroyed my life, I am still a student at BZU, completing my advanced research fellowship in the Department of Political Science.

Yet everything has changed.

I walk through the main gate of the university, past the brick pylons and the faded green Urdu motto: *Knowledge is Light.*

The four security guards in khaki uniforms are still sitting on plastic chairs under the tin canopy, drinking tea from glass cups. But when they see me approach, they do not tap their wooden batons against their shins. They do not block the path.

One of the senior guards stands up, touches the brim of his peaked cap in a crisp, respectful salute, and says:

"Assalam-o-Alaikum, Miss Anam. Good morning."

"Walaikum Assalam, Sub-Inspector," I reply with a calm, gracious smile, walking past without breaking my stride.

I do not lower my eyes. I do not look at his shoes. I look straight ahead down the eucalyptus boulevard.

Respect in a Pakistani university is not granted by the benevolence of the powerful; it is extracted through demonstrated, unassailable competence.

I walk across the campus toward Amna Hall.

The twelve-foot red brick boundary wall is still there, capped with its rusted razor wire and broken brown glass bottles. The massive green iron gate still stands sentinel over the gravel courtyard.

I step through the narrow wicket door.

Maasi Sakina is still sitting on her wooden charpai, wrapped in her faded ralli quilt, chewing her perpetual wad of tobacco.

When she hears my footsteps on the gravel, she looks up from her canvas ledger. Her yellowed eyes soften with that deep, maternal affection that peasant women reserve for survivors who refused to be broken.

"Jeeti raho, beti," she murmurs, spitting tobacco juice into her rusted tin can. "God give your pen long life."

"Shukriya, Maasi," I say, placing a small brown paper bag of fresh almond pastries from the bakery on Bosan Road onto her wooden table.

I climb the sixty-four concrete steps to the third floor of the East Wing.

Room 42 is still there, its warped wooden door scarred by thirty years of residents.

The Circle of Seven has scattered across the map of Pakistan, each woman carrying her unbowed sovereignty into the institutions of the state:

**Aqsa** is in Islamabad, working as an investigative political journalist for an independent digital news collective, writing the fearless exposés on public procurement fraud that she first dreamed of while printing *The Red Gate* on the Dean's duplicator.

**Anza** completed her degree with top honors; her family's printing shop in Bahawalpur has been rebuilt, and her younger sister entered university this autumn on an open merit scholarship, with her tuition paid in full by Anza's professional salary as a provincial social welfare officer.

**Saba** passed her federal competitive civil service examinations on her very first attempt, ranking third in the entire province; she is currently training at the Civil Services Academy in Lahore, carrying her econometric rigor into the corridors of the Ministry of Finance.

**Momina's** satirical caricatures were exhibited at the National College of Arts in Lahore to nationwide critical acclaim; she is now an independent visual artist and muralist whose massive, colorful public walls celebrate the labor of working-class women across Punjab.

**Sidra** is living in Lahore, completely free from the shadow of her uncle's forced marriage, working as an administrative coordinator for the women's legal collective that saved her life, helping other young women file their Section 164 affidavits of autonomy.

**Iqra** completed her Master's in Public Administration and launched a decentralized healthcare logistics initiative in southern Punjab, ensuring that rural clinics have emergency supplies of insulin and antibiotics that cannot be diverted by corrupt medical officers.

And **Palwasha** returned to her frontier homeland near Dera Ismail Khan, where she was appointed as the youngest female assistant commissioner in the tribal districts, enforcing the writ of constitutional law against feudal jirgas with her razor-sharp mind and her iron will.

And I remain in Room 42.

Every evening at seven, when the hostel curfew bar falls across the green gate, a soft knock sounds on our wooden door.

First-semester girls come to Room 42: young girls from small towns in Muzaffargarh, Layyah, and Rajanpur, their faces pale with anxiety, clutching their medical files, their thesis proposals, or their fee dispute slips.

"Baji, the professor told me to nod and say nothing. The warden threatened my father with a disciplinary letter. How do I survive this place without losing my mind?"

"Look at me," I say to each young woman. "I nodded until my neck broke. I nodded until I spent twelve months in the charity wards of Nishtar Hospital. I lost an entire year of my life to the lie that agreement keeps a woman safe."

She does not nod.

She smiles: a sharp, brilliant, dangerous smile of sovereign self-possession.

And in that small, twelve-by-fourteen room over the open sewer of Amna Hall, another unbroken woman is born into the world.
