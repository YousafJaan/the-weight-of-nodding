# Episode 25: Selling the Silver Anklet
### The Goldsmith's Scale, The Broken Vessel, and The Bank Counter

**Season 3 : The Mantle of Light** | Words: 909

> **Synopsis:** Smashing the clay piggy bank to count her savings, Rija realizes she is still four thousand rupees short of the MPhil fee, forcing a heartbreaking sacrifice : selling her grandmother's heirloom silver anklet to complete the challan.

---

The university admission deadline was set for Friday at four in the afternoon. On Wednesday evening, with Mother standing guard at the room door, I placed a clean white cotton cloth upon the floor, brought out the heavy clay bank from the chimney alcove, and struck its rounded crown with an iron pestle.
 
*CRACK.*
 
The vessel shattered into terracotta shards, unleashing a glorious avalanche of crumpled notes, copper coins, and silver five-rupee pieces across the cloth.
 
Mother and I spent two hours on our knees, meticulously sorting the currency : smoothing out the frayed edges of five-rupee bills, stacking ten-rupee notes into bundles of twenty, counting the coins into neat towers.
 
When the final total was tallied upon my notepad, my heart sank with a painful, hollow thud.
 
**Total Accumulated Savings : Nineteen Thousand, Six Hundred and Fifty Rupees.**
 
The required bank challan was exactly **Twenty-Four Thousand Rupees**.
 
I was four thousand, three hundred and fifty rupees short. And the fee was non-negotiable; the university admissions office rejected any application submitted with an incomplete deposit slip.
 
I sat upon the floor amid the piles of money, staring blankly at the numbers. Four thousand rupees was an impossible sum to generate in forty-eight hours in our village. Was it all for nothing? Would two years of relentless struggle, the starvation, the defiance against Taya Hakim Khan, all collapse for the lack of eighty dollars?
 
Mother looked at my stricken face, and her eyes filled with resolute determination.
 
Without saying a word, she reached down beneath the hem of her wide shalwar, unfastening the intricate silver hook of her left ankle.
 
She held out a thick, antique silver chain anklet : the *pazeeb* : adorned with delicate silver bells that had jingled on her feet on the day she entered our father's house as a seventeen-year-old bride.
 
"Mother, no!" I gasped, pushing her hand away. "Not your wedding anklet! It is the only jewelry you possess in this world!"
 
"Take it, Rija," she commanded, her voice fierce and unyielding as she pressed the heavy silver into my palm. "Silver is merely cold metal dug from the dirt. It has no value if it cannot purchase my daughter's freedom. Take it to the goldsmith in the tehsil bazaar tomorrow. Let this metal melt so your future may shine."
 
Hot tears spilled over my eyelids, soaking the crumpled five-rupee notes on the floor. I held that silver anklet against my chest, feeling the ancestral love of three generations of suffering women coursing through its links.

The following morning, I took the early passenger wagon to the tehsil bazaar, carrying the money bundled inside my canvas satchel and the silver anklet wrapped in tissue paper.
 
I walked into the narrow, mirror-lined shop of Lala Ramzan, the oldest goldsmith in the bazaar. He placed the antique anklet upon his brass balance scales, sliding small lead counterweights until the needles aligned.
 
"Antique work, daughter. Pure silver, ninety-five percent purity," the jeweler remarked, looking at me over his spectacles. "It weighs eight tolas. At current scrap rates, I can give you five thousand two hundred rupees."
 
"Give me the cash, Chacha," I replied without hesitation.
 
He counted out five crisp, brand-new thousand-rupee notes and two hundred-rupee bills.
 
I hurried across the crowded bazaar to the National Bank of Pakistan branch on Kachehri Road. The banking hall was packed with chaotic crowds of farmers, lawyers, and civil servants pushing against the iron teller grilles.
 
I filled out the four-copy university admission deposit slip with my antique silver fountain pen : **Account : Treasurer, Bahauddin Zakariya University Multan** 
**Applicant : Rija Sajjad** 
**Degree : MPhil Political Science & Constitutional Law** 
**Amount : Rs. 24,000 / - (Twenty-Four Thousand Rupees Only)**
 
When my turn arrived at Teller Window 3, I pushed the massive stack of currency : the five crisp notes from the goldsmith, along with the mountain of crumpled, sweat-stained five-, ten-, and fifty-rupee bills from thirty-five village mothers : through the iron bars.
 
The teller, a cynical young man with oiled hair, frowned with deep annoyance at the colossal pile of small-denomination notes.
 
"What is this nonsense, sister?" he complained loudly. "Do you expect me to spend an hour counting ten-rupee notes? Why could you not bring high-denomination bills?"
 
"Because, brother," I replied in a clear, resonant voice that silenced the surrounding queue, "this money was not stolen from a government treasury or earned through a corrupt bribe. It was earned penny by penny through honest labor by the poorest mothers of this district. Count it with respect."
 
The entire banking hall fell silent. The teller looked at my unwavering, dignified face, swallowed his insolence, and began counting the bills in silence.
 
Ten minutes later, the heavy brass stamp slammed down four times onto the carbon slips : *THUMP, THUMP, THUMP, THUMP*.
 
The teller slid the stamped applicant copy back across the marble counter.
 
I held that pink receipt in my hands. The purple ink of the official bank seal was still wet. Two thick tears rolled down my cheeks, splashing onto the paper, blurring the letter 'R' of my name.
 
That pink deposit slip was not a mere bank voucher; it was my declaration of emancipation, signed in the currency of honest toil and sealed with the melting of my mother's bridal silver.
 
I walked out of the bank into the blinding noon sunlight, and for the first time in my life, I felt completely, gloriously free.
