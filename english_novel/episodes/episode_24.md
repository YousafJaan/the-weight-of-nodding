# Episode 24: The Clay Piggy Bank
### The Earthen Vault, Counted Pennies, and The Treasure of Self-Reliance

**Season 3 : The Mantle of Light** | Words: 697

> **Synopsis:** Hoarding every coin earned from her tuition academy inside a small unglazed clay piggy bank, Rija guards her secret fund against family sabotage as the university admission deadline approaches.

---

To preserve my earnings in a household where every drawer was subject to arbitrary search by Hamza required extreme cunning.
 
From an itinerant potter who visited our village on a donkey cart, I purchased a small, unglazed earthen piggy bank : a simple clay sphere with a narrow, razor-thin slot cut into its crown, designed so that once a coin entered its hollow belly, it could only be retrieved by smashing the vessel to pieces.
 
I wrapped the clay bank in an old woolen sweater and concealed it deep inside the hollow space behind the mud bricks of the defunct chimney in my room, covering the opening with a framed print of an Islamic calligraphy verse.
 
Every evening, after the tuition children departed and the courtyard gate was locked, I would retreat to my room, light my small candle, and conduct the sacred ritual of the bank.
 
I would carefully smooth out the crumpled five- and ten-rupee notes on my wooden desktop, folding them into tight, paper-thin cylinders. Then, one by one, I would slide them through the clay slit : *clink, clink, flutter*.
 
Every single coin represented an hour of teaching under the scorching sun; every folded note represented a victory over dependence.
 
Sitting in the candlelight, listening to the hollow echo of the coins growing duller as the clay vessel slowly filled, I felt a deep, intoxicating surge of autonomy. In our patriarchal society, women are systematically kept ignorant of financial mechanics precisely to maintain their servitude. A woman who knows how to earn, save, and budget five hundred rupees is infinitely more dangerous to the feudal order than a woman who simply knows how to read French poetry.
 
One evening, while I was sliding a fifty-rupee note into the slot, the wooden door suddenly rattled!

Hamza had kicked the door, pushing it open before I could extinguish the candle!
 
With lightning-fast presence of mind, I shoved the clay bank behind my pillow and spread my open notebook across the desk.
 
Hamza stood in the doorway, his eyes darting suspiciously across the small room. He had been sent by Aunt Shabana, who had noticed the influx of village children into our courtyard and smelled money.
 
"What are you hiding, Rija?" he demanded, walking over to the desk, his heavy hands rummaging through my textbook pile. "I hear you are running an illegal business in our courtyard. Taya Hakim Khan says you are collecting fees from low-caste laborers. Hand over the money. All revenue generated upon our family land belongs to the male head of household."
 
I leaned back in my wooden chair, crossing my arms over my chest, looking at him with that ice-cold, fearless gaze that always unsettled him.
 
"Search the room, Hamza," I said calmly. "Tear up the floorboards. Throw the charpai into the courtyard. But remember : those children are being taught in my private quarters with Mother's permission. If you lay a single finger on a coin meant for their examination slates, I will walk straight to the village primary school tomorrow and inform the headmaster that Sajjad's son is stealing twenty-rupee coins from poor widows. Let us see how your 'ghairat' survives that public humiliation."
 
Hamza's face flushed with furious embarrassment. He knew that the village schoolmaster was a respected elder whose word carried immense weight. He spat onto the floorboards, kicked my wastepaper basket across the room, and cursed loudly.
 
"You think you are clever, don't you?" he hissed. "Keep your miserable beggar pennies! You will never raise twenty thousand rupees for your university fees. You will rot in this courtyard!"
 
He slammed the door and stormed off.
 
I sat in the dark for a long time, my heart racing, but my hands steady. I retrieved the clay sphere from behind the pillow and held its cool, rough surface against my cheek.
 
"He is wrong, my Lord," I whispered into the darkness. "He is wrong. With Your help, this clay vessel will break the gates of their fortress."
 
By the final week of August, as the university entrance notification arrived in the mail, the clay bank had grown heavy as an iron cannonball. The moment of reckoning had arrived.
