# Episode 42: The Red Carpet at Convention Centre
### The Avenues of Islamabad, The Crystal Chandeliers, and The Invisible Assembly

**Season 5 : The Turban and the Dawn** | Words: 501

> **Synopsis:** Arriving in Islamabad under state protocol, Rija enters the grand Jinnah Convention Centre, feeling the spiritual presence of every disinherited daughter walking beside her onto the red carpet.

---

The transition from the dusty, narrow mud alleys of Southern Punjab to the grand, manicured avenues of Islamabad felt like crossing into an entirely different dimension of reality.
 
On Monday afternoon, an official government staff car escorted Mother, Father, and me to the State Guest House on Constitution Avenue. The Margalla Hills rose majestic and green against the crystal-clear winter sky, their peaks dusted with fresh mountain air.
 
The following morning, our car pulled up to the grand ceremonial entrance of the Jinnah Convention Centre.
 
The monumental domed amphitheater was a masterpiece of modern state architecture. Outside, brass military bands in ceremonial scarlet uniforms played national anthems, while white-gloved protocol officers escorted arriving dignitaries : federal cabinet ministers, foreign ambassadors in business suits, Supreme Court justices in formal black robes, military generals with rows of medals, and international television crews broadcasting live across satellite networks.
 
When the protocol officer opened our car door, Father stepped out onto the plush crimson carpet, his white ancestral turban towering proudly above his head, holding Mother's hand.
 
I stepped out behind them.
 
I wore a simple, elegant dark-green silk abaya with embroidered ivory cuffs, and my mother's white cotton chador draped gracefully over my head and shoulders.
 
As we walked down the grand entrance foyer beneath soaring ceilings hung with multi-tiered crystal chandeliers, flanked by television cameras and clicking press photographers, an extraordinary, sacred sensation enveloped my spirit.

I looked down at the crimson carpet beneath my leather shoes.
 
I knew that I was not walking alone onto that national stage.
 
Walking invisibly beside me was Saba, with her bruised fingers and her quiet courage.
 
Walking beside me was Sara, with her fierce, burning defiance against feudal hypocrisy.
 
Walking beside me was Aqsa, whose green spiral notebook had been trampled in the gravel of Bosan Road, whose stolen voice was now demanding justice through my tongue.
 
And walking behind us were the thousands of anonymous, barefoot rural daughters across the cotton fields of the Chenab and the Indus : the young widows forced to renounce their inheritance at gunpoint, the girls locked inside dark storerooms, the women who wept silently over clay stoves in the dead of night.
 
I was their ambassador. I was their voice. I was the proof that their suffering was not in vain.
 
A senior protocol director escorted us to our reserved front-row seats in the VIP auditorium, directly opposite the grand mahogany stage adorned with massive bronze national crests and fresh white floral arrangements.
 
Father sat beside me, his back straight, his eyes scanning the glittering assembly of ministers and generals. He turned to me and whispered, his voice trembling with unspeakable pride : "Rija, look at this hall. The rulers of this nation are seated here to honor my daughter."
 
"No, Father," I whispered back, taking his hand. "They are seated here to honor the truth."
 
At eleven sharp, the trumpet fanfare sounded, and the entire assembly rose to its feet as the President of Pakistan entered the hall.
