# Episode 08: Scorched Roofs and Starlit Vows
### The 47-Degree Crucible, Blackout Nights, and The Cosmic Sanctuary

**Season 1 : The Sanctuary and the Iron Gate** | Words: 733

> **Synopsis:** As the brutal heatwave of Southern Punjab strikes Multan with fourteen-hour power blackouts, the residents of Aisha Hall flee to the vast open roof, where Rija finds cosmic peace and spiritual renewal beneath the desert stars.

---

By late May and early June, the ancient city of Multan transformed into a blazing atmospheric kiln. The sun rose at six in the morning like an incandescent ball of white fury, radiating waves of heat so ferocious that the asphalt on Bosan Road softened into tar, trapping the sandals of passing pedestrians. The hot, dry desert winds : the legendary *Loo* of the Southern plains : swept across the barren fields, carrying scorching dust devils that stripped the moisture from human eyes within seconds.
 
The red-brick masonry of Aisha Hall, saturated with months of relentless solar radiation, began to behave like the interior walls of a brickmaker's furnace. By two in the afternoon, touching the interior plaster with an open palm resulted in an involuntary recoil from the heat.
 
To compound this torment, the regional electrical grid collapsed under the peak summer surge, plunging the university into twelve to fourteen hours of continuous load-shedding every day. Inside Room 42, the air became so thick, dead, and superheated that drawing a breath felt like inhaling steam from a boiling kettle.
 
Final semester examinations were barely three weeks away. I sat at my wooden desk with a wet cotton towel wrapped around my shorn head, desperately attempting to memorize judicial precedents. But within eight minutes, the drenched towel dried into stiff, brittle cardboard. Sweat dripped steadily from my eyelashes, splashing onto the white paper, dissolving the blue fountain-pen ink into blurry, illegible smudges. The throbbing ache behind my temples was relentless, and nosebleeds became a daily occurrence among the exhausted residents.
 
During the afternoons, the central hallway resembled an emergency casualty ward : dozens of young women lay motionless on the bare terrazzo floor in semi-conscious stupors, their cotton kurtas drenched in perspiration, feebly waving pieces of cardboard to fend off the swarming flies.

The sole salvation of Aisha Hall lay not within its suffocating brick rooms, but above them : upon the immense, open concrete expanse of the hostel roof.
 
Every evening at ten, the moment the final night roll call was completed, an extraordinary exodus began. Hundreds of young women : laden with cotton mattresses, frayed quilts, pillows, water jugs, and textbooks : climbed the narrow iron spiral staircase leading to the sky.
 
Up on that vast, wind-swept rooftop, three stories above the burning earth, the desert wind finally offered a cool, merciful embrace. Across that sea of concrete, hundreds of white cotton sheets were spread side by side, creating a sprawling, ethereal encampment of dreaming daughters.
 
I would lie on my back upon my thin cotton sheet, my head resting upon my bundled clothes, staring upward into the infinite, ink-black canopy of the Punjab night. Far away from the blinding neon glare of modern metropolises, the sky above Multan blazed with millions of diamond-bright stars, the Milky Way arching across the cosmos like a river of silver dust.
 
In that vast cosmic silence, the petty miseries of Aisha Hall dissolved into irrelevance. The malicious whispers of Umme Rubab, the bureaucratic cruelty of Miss Nazia, the humiliating arguments over stale bread : all of it shrank into microscopic insignificance before the majesty of the celestial order.
 
I would softly recite the verses of Surah Al-Mulk beneath my breath : *'Who created the seven heavens in layers. You do not see in the creation of the Most Merciful any inconsistency. So turn your vision again; do you see any flaw?'*
 
Lying beneath that starry expanse, I realized that the trials of this world are not designed to destroy the believer; they are designed to shatter the false illusions of worldly dependence. Every tear that fell from my eyes onto that rough concrete was absorbed by the earth, but the resolve hardening inside my soul belonged to the heavens.
 
"Look at that falling star, Rija," Sara whispered beside me, her silhouette dark against the celestial glow. "Make a wish. Perhaps the universe is listening tonight."
 
"I do not wish upon falling rocks, Sara," I replied with a faint, peaceful smile. "I pray to the One who threw them across the sky. And my prayer is simple : to grant me the strength to stand tall until the morning comes."
 
That scorching rooftop was my cathedral. In the heart of Multan's inferno, beneath the silent gaze of the desert stars, a daughter's wounded soul found the divine sanctuary that no human cruelty could ever violate.
