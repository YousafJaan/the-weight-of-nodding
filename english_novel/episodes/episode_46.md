# Episode 46: The Morning Fog at the Railway Platform
### The Return on Shalimar Express, The Waiting Crowds, and The Paternal Silhouette

**Season 5 : The Turban and the Dawn** | Words: 509

> **Synopsis:** Returning by train to the rural hometown station at dawn, Rija arrives to find hundreds of villagers, schoolteachers, and elders waiting with brass bands and flower garlands to welcome her.

---

The overnight journey on the Shalimar Express from Rawalpindi back to Southern Punjab was a surreal, dreamlike passage through the awakening heartland of Pakistan.
 
Inside our first-class private coupe : arranged through the courtesy of the Prime Minister's Secretariat : Mother slept peacefully beneath her woolen shawl, while Father sat by the vibrating window for ten hours straight, his gaze fixed on the passing telegraph poles and moonlit fields, occasionally reaching over to gently touch the green velvet box containing the National Gold Medal resting on the table.
 
At six-thirty in the morning, the heavy iron brakes of the express train began to screech, slowing to a gentle halt alongside the concrete platform of our small rural hometown station.
 
A dense, pearlescent morning fog hovered over the tracks, glowing with the first pale amber rays of the rising sun.
 
I pulled my white chador securely over my head, adjusted my coat, and prepared to step out into the freezing winter air.
 
The moment the conductor swung open the heavy steel carriage door, a deafening roar of beating drums and brass trumpets shattered the morning quiet!
 
*DHOL-DHOL-DHOL-DHOL!*
 
I stepped onto the platform footboard, and the sight that greeted my eyes took the breath completely from my lungs.
 
The entire railway station was packed with over five hundred people!
 
There were the teachers and headmasters from three surrounding rural schools, wearing their formal coats; there were delegations of young female college students holding hand-painted banners that read : **'WELCOME HOME, NATIONAL HERO SCHOLAR RIJA SAJJAD!'**; there were the village shopkeepers, the tenant farmers, the tractor drivers, and dozens of young children throwing fresh red rose petals into the air!

The local member of the provincial assembly stepped forward, accompanied by the Deputy Superintendent of Police and the station master, placing an immense garland of fresh tuberoses and golden tinsel around my neck.
 
Photographers from the regional newspapers jostled for position, their flashes illuminating the morning mist.
 
The very station where I had boarded the battered passenger vans in tears four years prior, hiding my face in fear of being recognized, had now become the portal of a state triumph.
 
Yet, amid the swirling storm of rose petals, the deafening drums, and the cheering crowds, my eyes searched for only one person.
 
There, standing at the very edge of the platform beside a weathered brick pillar, standing slightly apart from the politicians and the cameras, was Father.
 
He had stepped down from the train carriage a few moments ahead of me.
 
His tall, lean frame was trembling visibly. He wore his spotless white cotton kurta and his black waistcoat, his white ancestral turban wound tightly above his weathered brow.
 
His hands were clasped in front of his chest, and his eyes were locked onto my face.
 
Across the twenty yards of crowded concrete separating us, through the cloud of falling red petals and morning mist, our gazes met.
 
The time for speeches was over. The historical debt of a father to his daughter was about to be paid before the entire world.
