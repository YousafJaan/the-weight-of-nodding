# Episode 32: The Thunderstorm in the Archive
### The Monsoon Deluge, The Leaking Roof, and Defending the Manuscript

**Season 4 : The Crucible of Intellect** | Words: 622

> **Synopsis:** A catastrophic monsoon storm strikes Multan, cutting power and flooding the library archives, forcing Rija to physically shield decades-old legal ledgers and her master draft from destruction.

---

In late July, the monsoon arrived over Southern Punjab not with gentle summer rain, but with the apocalyptic fury of an airborne ocean. Dark, violet-black storm clouds rolled across the Chenab basin at three in the afternoon, transforming the daylight into an eerie, bruised twilight before unleashing a deluge of torrential rain and gale-force winds that tore branches from the university's century-old eucalyptus trees.
 
Inside the third-floor constitutional archives of the Central Library, the sound of the rain slamming against the high glass windows was deafening, like thousands of gravel stones hurled against crystal.
 
Suddenly, a blinding flash of lightning illuminated the hall, followed instantly by an ear-splitting crack of thunder that rattled the heavy chandeliers.
 
The lights flickered violently twice, and then extinguished completely.
 
The regional grid had collapsed. The library was plunged into total, inky blackness.
 
Within minutes, a new and terrifying sound emerged above the roar of the downpour : the steady, frantic dripping of water from the ceiling. The old flat roof of the library, neglected by university maintenance for years, had sprung multiple severe leaks directly above the rare document stacks!
 
A stream of brown, muddy rainwater began cascading directly onto Stack 17 : the very shelf holding the unique, irreplaceable 1920 district land revenue settlement registers and my own handwritten field survey files!
 
"Chacha Bashir!" I screamed into the darkness, switching on the small LED flashlight of my mobile phone. "The roof is leaking! The archives are drowning!"
 
I lunged across the dark aisle, heedless of the slippery floor tiles.

What followed was an hour of desperate, frantic salvage in the dark.
 
Chacha Bashir arrived with two plastic buckets and an old canvas tarp. Working side by side beneath the beam of my flashlight, with water splashing over our heads and soaking our clothes to the skin, we hauled hundreds of heavy, waterlogged folios from the high shelves, carrying them across the flooded floor to the dry sanctuary of the central reading hall.
 
My master draft : three hundred and twenty pages of meticulously typed and annotated thesis text, representing two full years of my life : was sitting on Desk 14, barely three feet from an expanding puddle of water.
 
I stripped off my heavy black wool chador, wrapped it securely around the stack of manuscripts, and clutched the bundle tightly against my chest, shielding it with my own body as plaster dust and rainwater rained down from the cracked ceiling.
 
By nine in the evening, the storm had passed, leaving the library quiet save for the steady drip of buckets.
 
Chacha Bashir and I sat upon the dry marble steps of the ground-floor lobby, soaked to our undergarments, shivering with cold, our hands black with ancient book soot and plaster.
 
He looked at me clutching my chador-wrapped manuscript, and he chuckled softly through his wet grey beard.
 
"You are a fierce one, Bibi," he smiled, wringing out the hem of his cotton kurta. "Most students would have run down the stairs to save their shoes. You stood in the deluge to save hundred-year-old papers and your own writings."
 
"A degree can be reprinted on fresh paper, Chacha ji," I replied, carefully inspecting the dry edges of my draft within the wool folds. "But the recorded cries of disinherited women cannot be replaced. If these records had drowned tonight, their truth would have drowned with them."
 
Through the tall glass portal of the lobby, the moon broke through the ragged edges of the departing storm clouds, casting a pure, silver beam of light across the wet marble floor.
 
I knew then with absolute certainty : no storm of nature, and no storm of human malice, could ever extinguish the flame that burned within my chest.
