# Episode 22: Rising from the Ashes
### The Morning Ultimatum, The Sovereign Daughter, and The MPhil Resolve

**Season 3 : The Mantle of Light** | Words: 687

> **Synopsis:** Rija confronts Father in the morning courtyard, presenting her taped-together degree and declaring her irrevocable intention to pursue an MPhil at BZU without asking for a single rupee of his money.

---

The sun rose the following morning like a golden disc cutting through the dawn mist of Southern Punjab. The courtyard was washed in pale amber light, the air crisp with the refreshing cool of early dawn.
 
Father was seated at the edge of the brick veranda, his white hair disheveled, slowly sipping lukewarm tea from an enamel cup.
 
I walked out of my room.
 
In my hands, I carried two documents : first, my university graduation certificate, which I had spent two hours carefully piecing back together with clear cellophane tape, the jagged tear lines running across its gold university crest like battle scars; and second, the newly printed admission brochure and bank voucher for the MPhil degree program in Political Science and Constitutional Law at Bahauddin Zakariya University.
 
I walked across the courtyard gravel with slow, measured, deliberate strides, coming to a halt directly before Father's rope charpai.
 
Father looked up. When his tired, bloodshot eyes saw me standing tall, wrapped in Mother's white chador, holding that repaired certificate, an involuntary spasm of guilt and pain crossed his weathered face.
 
"Father," I said. My voice was completely free of anger, yet it possessed a granite firmness that made him set his tea cup down onto the wooden bench.
 
"Rija..." he began, his voice hoarse, looking down at his calloused hands. "The village... your brother... I never wished for you to be locked in that room..."
 
"I know what you wished, Father," I interrupted gently. "You wished for peace, and you believed that sacrificing your daughter's life was the cheapest price to purchase the good opinion of cowards. But that bargain has failed."
 
I placed the repaired certificate and the admission brochure onto the charpai beside him.

Father stared at the documents, his eyebrows furrowing in deep perplexity. "What is this, Rija?"
 
"This is the admission prospectus for the Master of Philosophy program at the university," I answered clearly. "The national entrance examination takes place next month. I intend to sit for that exam, secure admission on merit, and complete my postgraduate research."
 
Father gasped, shaking his head with sudden, terrified disbelief. "Are you mad, Rija? After everything that has transpired? After the slanders, the broken matches, the threats from Taya Hakim Khan? You want to return to Multan? Do you want to see me killed?"
 
"Listen to me carefully, Father," I said, stepping closer, looking into his eyes with absolute sovereignty. "I am not asking for your permission. Nor am I asking for a single rupee of your money. I will not ask you for bus fare, for book expenses, or for tuition fees. I will earn every single coin with my own hands, my own labor, and my own intellect."
 
Father stared at me as if looking upon a total stranger.
 
"If I ever bring a single genuine stain of moral dishonor to this house," I continued, "you have my full permission to take Hamza's shotgun and execute me in the center of this courtyard. But if I return victorious with a master's degree and the highest honors of this nation, you will take off that white turban and you will place it upon my head."
 
Father's lips trembled violently. He opened his mouth to shout, to assert his ancient patriarchal prerogative, to issue a furious command. But as he looked at my steady, unblinking eyes, at the quiet nobility radiating from my countenance, his voice died in his throat.
 
He saw before him not an obedient child who could be terrorized into silence, but an adult woman whose spirit had been forged in the crucible of death.
 
He slowly reached out, his trembling fingers lightly brushing the cellophane tape across my torn certificate. A single, heavy tear escaped his eyelid, rolling down into his grey beard.
 
Without uttering a word, he picked up his walking stick, rose from the charpai, and walked slowly into the street.
 
Mother stepped out from the kitchen doorway, wiping her hands on her apron, smiling through her tears.
 
The declaration of war had been delivered, and the terms of engagement were set. Now, the battle for economic self-reliance began.
